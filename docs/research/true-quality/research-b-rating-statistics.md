# The statistics of debiasing ratings — research findings

## Executive summary

1. **A raw average is a point estimate with no uncertainty attached — never sort or trust it without also looking at *n*.** A 5.0 average from 3 ratings is statistically weaker evidence than a 4.6 average from 3,000. The fix is not "more stars wins" but computing a **lower confidence/credible bound** on the true rating and comparing *that*.
2. **For binary/thumbs signals**, use the **Wilson score interval lower bound** (Wilson 1927, popularized by Evan Miller 2009) — closed-form, computable by hand from (positive, n). For **5-star histograms**, the correct analogue is a **Dirichlet-multinomial credible interval** (Evan Miller 2014) computed from the 5-bucket histogram, not the mean alone — Wilson's formula itself does *not* apply to star data.
3. **Small-n perfect scores should be pulled toward a prior**, not trusted at face value: Laplace/additive smoothing, IMDb's public weighted-rating formula (`WR=(v/(v+m))R+(m/(v+m))C`, official current `m=25,000`), and James–Stein/empirical-Bayes shrinkage are three formalizations of the same idea, differing in how principled vs. ad hoc the "how much to shrink" choice is.
4. **The J-shaped (bimodal, mostly-5-mostly-1) distribution is the *normal, honest* shape of review data**, not evidence of manipulation — it's produced by two well-documented self-selection biases (acquisition bias, under-reporting/"brag-or-moan" bias), per Hu, Pavlou & Zhang's peer-reviewed body of work. An agent should not treat J-shape alone as a fraud signal.
5. **What *is* evidence of manipulation** is quantified in the literature only weakly and mostly qualitatively: incentivized reviews shift a product's mean by roughly **+0.38 stars** on average but can jump its **percentile rank from ~54th to ~94th** (Amazon industry analysis); purchased fake reviews on Facebook groups produce a **short-term** rating bump that reverses (He, Hollenbeck & Proserpio, *Marketing Science* 2022). No peer-reviewed source gives a specific numeric "5★:4★ ratio" or "missing-middle" cutoff — treat those as heuristics, not thresholds.
6. **A large fraction of who rates at all is systematically non-random**: extreme-experience self-selection, social-influence herding (a single early upvote inflates final aggregate ratings by ~25%, Muchnik/Aral/Taylor, *Science* 2013), reciprocity/retaliation in two-sided marketplaces (Airbnb, eBay), cross-platform rating-scale culture differences (US raters use extremes ~41% more than Japanese raters), and **survivorship bias**: badly-rated listings get delisted/closed over time (empirically, lower-Yelp-rated restaurants close at significantly higher rates — Luca & Luca, NBER WP 25806), so the population of *currently visible* long-lived listings is pre-filtered toward survivors, inflating the apparent base rate of quality.
7. **Ratings are written in the "honeymoon window"**, not after years of ownership — Consumer Reports' own reliability methodology explicitly models a 5-year (2-year for phones) failure window that user reviews, written mostly at unboxing, cannot see.
8. **When combining heterogeneous sources** (a lab spec sheet, a user-rating average, a professional review), use **inverse-variance weighting**, and — critically — keep *variance* (shrinks with n) and *bias* (does not shrink with n, no matter how many biased ratings you add) analytically separate. A small precise measurement can and should outweigh a huge but structurally biased or noisy crowd average.
9. **Every threshold quoted below is sourced**; where none exists (5★:4★ ratio cutoffs, Benford's law on star digits, universal "N reviews is enough"), it is flagged explicitly in Dead Ends and Myths.

## Findings

### F1. Naive sorting/ranking rules are provably wrong
- **Claim:** Sorting by (positive − negative) or by raw average both produce absurd rankings under realistic vote counts.
- **Mechanism:** Net-difference sorting lets huge-n items with a lower positive *rate* outrank small-n items with a higher rate; raw-average sorting lets a single 2-for-2 item outrank a 100-for-101 item because it ignores sample size entirely.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** Given `positive`/`negative` counts (or a 5-star histogram), never rank purely by `positive − negative` or by `mean`. Compute a lower-bound estimate instead (F2/F3).
- **Strength:** Practitioner-canonical, math peer-reviewed (draws on Wilson 1927 and Agresti & Coull 1998, *The American Statistician*).
- **Source(s):** Evan Miller, "How Not To Sort By Average Rating" (Feb 6, 2009), https://www.evanmiller.org/how-not-to-sort-by-average-rating.html
- **Caveats:** This is about *ranking/comparison*, not about detecting fraud; a low-n item can still be genuinely great, just unproven.

### F2. Wilson score interval lower bound (binary up/down ratings)
- **Claim:** The 95%-confidence lower bound on the true positive-rating fraction is the correct way to compare items with different sample sizes on a binary (helpful/not, thumbs up/down, recommend/don't) scale.
- **Mechanism:** It's the lower bound of the Wilson (1927) confidence interval for a Bernoulli proportion, derived by inverting the normal approximation to the binomial test statistic; unlike the Wald/normal interval, it doesn't break down near 0% or 100%.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** With `p̂ = positive/n`, `n` = total ratings, `z` = the (1−α/2) quantile of the standard normal (`z=1.96` for 95% confidence):

  `p_W = [ p̂ + z²/(2n) − z·√( (p̂(1−p̂) + z²/(4n)) / n ) ] / (1 + z²/n)`

  Assumptions: ratings are i.i.d. Bernoulli trials from a fixed underlying "true" positive rate (no drift, no correlated voters — herding/reciprocity violate this, see F12/F14); normal approximation to the binomial (reasonably good for n≥~10).
- **Strength:** Peer-reviewed derivation (Wilson 1927, *JASA*; Agresti & Coull 1998, *The American Statistician*), Evan Miller's write-up is the field-standard explainer, adopted in production by Reddit and Yelp per his post.
- **Source(s):** Evan Miller, "How Not To Sort By Average Rating," https://www.evanmiller.org/how-not-to-sort-by-average-rating.html; E.B. Wilson (1927), "Probable Inference, the Law of Succession, and Statistical Inference," *Journal of the American Statistical Association*, 22, 209–212; Agresti, A. & Coull, B.A. (1998), "Approximate is Better than 'Exact' for Interval Estimation of Binomial Proportions," *The American Statistician*, 52, 119–126.
- **Worked example (classic, reproduced from the formula, z=1.96):**
  - Item A: 2 positive, 0 negative (n=2). Raw mean = **1.00 (100%)**. Wilson lower bound = **0.342**.
  - Item B: 100 positive, 1 negative (n=101). Raw mean = **0.990 (99%)**. Wilson lower bound = **0.946**.
  - Despite A's higher raw average, B's lower bound is far higher (0.946 vs 0.342) — B is the better-evidenced item. [Computed directly from the formula above; verified numerically.]
- **Caveats / false positives:** Only valid for two-outcome data. Applying it directly to a 5-star mean (treating the mean as if it were a proportion) is a common but statistically invalid shortcut — see Dead Ends.

### F3. Dirichlet-multinomial credible interval for K-star histograms
- **Claim:** For 5-star (or K-star) systems, the statistically correct analogue of Wilson's bound uses the full rating histogram (`n_1..n_K`, counts at each star level), not just the mean, via a Bayesian credible interval under a Dirichlet prior.
- **Mechanism:** Put a flat Dirichlet(1,...,1) prior over the probability vector `p=(p_1,...,p_K)` of a rater giving each star level; the posterior after observing counts `(n_1,...,n_K)` is Dirichlet(`n_1+1,...,n_K+1`). The average-rating function `f(p)=Σ s_k p_k` (with `s_k=k` points) is linear in `p`, so its posterior mean and variance follow directly from Dirichlet moments, and a normal approximation gives a credible interval.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** With `N=Σn_k` total ratings, `K` star levels, `s_k=k`:

  `S = Σ_k s_k·(n_k+1)/(N+K) − z·√[ ( Σ_k s_k²·(n_k+1)/(N+K) − ( Σ_k s_k·(n_k+1)/(N+K) )² ) / (N+K+1) ]`

  `z=1.65` for a 90% one-sided bound, `z=1.96` for 95%. The first term (before the minus) is the shrunk (Bayes-adjusted) mean; the subtracted term is `z` times the estimated standard deviation of that mean. Rank/compare items on `S`, not on the raw mean. Same formula also gives a rule for whether to *display* an average at all: don't show a rating to the nearest half-star unless `2×z×σ < 0.5`.
- **Strength:** Peer-reviewed-grade derivation (published methodological blog post with a full worked mathematical appendix; not itself peer-reviewed, but the underlying Dirichlet-multinomial and normal-approximation machinery is standard Bayesian statistics).
- **Source(s):** Evan Miller, "Ranking Items With Star Ratings: An Approximate Bayesian Approach" (Sept 16, 2014), https://www.evanmiller.org/ranking-items-with-star-ratings.html
- **Worked example — the exact comparison the task requested (z=1.96, 95%):**
  - **Item A**: n=23, histogram roughly 21×5★ + 2×4★ → raw mean = **4.91**. Credible-interval calc: shrunk mean = 4.571, variance = 0.0331, **lower bound S_A ≈ 4.215**.
  - **Item B**: n=4,100, histogram ≈ 82×1★ + 123×2★ + 246×3★ + 779×4★ + 2,870×5★ → raw mean = **4.52**. Shrunk mean = 4.518, variance = 0.000193, **lower bound S_B ≈ 4.491**.
  - Despite A's higher raw average (4.91 vs 4.52), **B has the higher, more defensible lower bound (4.491 vs 4.215)** — B should rank/be trusted above A. [All values computed directly from the formula above with the stated histograms; the histograms are illustrative constructions consistent with the stated n and mean, not scraped real data — an agent should build the actual histogram from the product page rather than assume one.]
  - Note the general shape from Miller's derivation: the number of ratings needed to reach a given interval width scales **quadratically** with target width when ratings are spread out (uniform case: `N ≈ 8z²/w² − 6`), but only **linearly** when ratings already agree with each other (consensus case: `N ≈ 11z/w − 5.5`) — i.e., a handful of ratings can be "enough" only when they closely agree with each other, not merely when they're numerous.
- **Caveats / false positives:** Uses a flat/uninformative Dirichlet(1,...,1) prior, which is itself a modeling choice — the true empirical prior for online reviews is J-shaped, not uniform (see F8), so this formula is somewhat conservative but not maximally efficient. Also a normal approximation to a Dirichlet-derived quantity, which degrades for very small N or extreme skew.

### F4. Jeffreys and Agresti–Coull intervals — alternatives to Wilson for binary proportions
- **Claim:** Wilson's interval is one of several "good" alternatives to the naive Wald (normal-approximation) interval for a binomial proportion; Jeffreys and Agresti–Coull are close cousins with different theoretical motivations and near-identical practical behavior.
- **Mechanism:** *Jeffreys interval* is Bayesian: put a Jeffreys prior Beta(1/2,1/2) on the proportion; the posterior is Beta(`x+1/2, n−x+1/2`); the interval is the equal-tailed `α/2`/`1−α/2` quantiles of that posterior Beta. *Agresti–Coull* is a corrected-Wald approach: add `z²` "pseudo-observations" split evenly, i.e. use `ñ = n+z²`, `p̃ = (x+z²/2)/ñ`, then the ordinary normal interval `p̃ ± z·√(p̃(1−p̃)/ñ)`. For a 95% interval (`z≈2`) this reduces to the well-known `p̃=(x+2)/(n+4)`.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:**
  - Jeffreys: `Jeffreys(x,n,α) = ( Q_{α/2}(x+½, n−x+½), Q_{1−α/2}(x+½, n−x+½) )` where `Q` is the Beta-distribution quantile function.
  - Agresti–Coull: `ñ=n+z²_{α/2}`, `p̃=(x+z²_{α/2}/2)/ñ`, interval `= p̃ ± z_{α/2}√(p̃(1−p̃)/ñ)`. Note the Agresti–Coull midpoint `p̃` is exactly the posterior mean under a **Beta(2,2)** prior — i.e. it's a specific, mild instance of Bayesian shrinkage (F5).
- **Strength:** Peer-reviewed (Agresti & Coull 1998, *The American Statistician*; Brown, Cai & DasGupta 2001, *Statistical Science*, is the standard comparative reference, not separately verified here but widely cited for the coverage properties).
- **Source(s):** Wikipedia's *Binomial proportion confidence interval* article as an index, cross-checked against: SAS Global Forum paper on the Jeffreys interval, https://support.sas.com/resources/papers/proceedings15/3020-2015.pdf; `stests` R package docs on Agresti–Coull, https://fhernanb.github.io/stests/reference/ci_p_agresti_coull.html
- **Caveats / false positives:** For an agent doing back-of-envelope comparisons, Wilson, Jeffreys, and Agresti–Coull give near-identical lower bounds for n≳20; below that they can diverge meaningfully. There is no evidence any one is definitively "more correct" for rating-sorting purposes — Wilson is simply the one with an established track record in this exact application (Reddit, Yelp).

### F5. Laplace/additive smoothing and informative Beta-Binomial shrinkage
- **Claim:** The simplest way to avoid a small-n perfect score dominating a naive average is to add "pretend" observations to both numerator and denominator before computing the mean.
- **Mechanism:** This is the Bayesian posterior mean under a Beta(`α,β`) prior on a Bernoulli/binary rate: with a flat Beta(1,1) prior this is Laplace's classical "add-one" rule of succession; with an informative prior centered on the category/platform average and a chosen "prior strength" `m`, it becomes a general-purpose Bayesian average.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:**
  - Flat (add-one): `p_smoothed = (positive + 1) / (n + 2)`
  - Informative prior (mean `μ_prior`, strength `m` = "worth of m pseudo-ratings"): `p_smoothed = (positive + μ_prior·m) / (n + m)`
- **Strength:** Textbook Bayesian statistics (conjugate Beta-Binomial update); the specific application to rating systems is Evan Miller's write-up, itself a special case of the same math used in F6 (IMDb) and F3.
- **Source(s):** Evan Miller, "Bayesian Average Ratings" (Nov 6, 2012), https://www.evanmiller.org/bayesian-average-ratings.html
- **Worked example (same two items as F2, using flat add-one smoothing, α=β=1):**
  - Item A (2/2): raw = 1.00 → smoothed = (2+1)/(2+2) = **0.750**
  - Item B (100/101): raw = 0.990 → smoothed = (100+1)/(101+2) = **0.981**
  - With an *informative* prior set to the platform's overall positive rate (e.g. `μ_prior=0.80`, `m=10` pseudo-ratings): A → (2+8)/(2+10) = **0.833**; B → (100+8)/(101+10) = **0.973**. [Computed directly from the formulas above.]
- **Caveats / false positives:** The choice of `m` (how many pseudo-observations to add) and `μ_prior` (what to shrink toward) is a modeling decision with real consequences — too large an `m` erases genuine signal from moderately-sized samples; there's no universal "correct" `m,` only application-specific tuning (IMDb's choice, F6, is one concrete precedent).

### F6. IMDb's public weighted-rating (Bayesian average) formula
- **Claim:** IMDb's Top-250 ranking uses a documented, fixed-prior Bayesian average that shrinks low-vote titles toward the sitewide mean rating.
- **Mechanism:** Same Beta/Bayesian-average logic as F5, applied with a large, fixed prior-strength parameter `m` (currently the minimum vote count for chart eligibility) and prior mean `C` = the mean rating across all rated titles.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** `WR = (v/(v+m))·R + (m/(v+m))·C`, where `R` = the title's own raw mean rating, `v` = number of votes it has, `m` = 25,000 (IMDb's currently documented minimum-votes threshold for Top-250 eligibility, as of the article's Feb 9, 2026 update), `C` = the mean rating across the whole eligible pool (IMDb does not publish the exact live value of `C`, but the site's own scale historically centers around ~7.0).
- **Strength:** Vendor methodology, but **officially published and dated** by IMDb itself (rare for a live consumer platform) — treat as "vendor-disclosed ground truth," not third-party inference.
- **Source(s):** IMDb Help, "Ratings FAQ" (updated 9-Feb-2026), https://help.imdb.com/article/imdb/track-movies-tv/ratings-faq/G67Y87TFYYP6TWAV — explicitly states the formula, `m=25,000`, and that `R`/`v`/`C` are as defined.
- **Worked example (`m=25,000`, illustrative `C=7.0`, since IMDb does not publish the live value):**
  - Niche film: `v=500`, `R=9.5` → `WR = (500/25500)·9.5 + (25000/25500)·7.0 = 7.05`
  - Blockbuster: `v=250,000`, `R=8.0` → `WR = (250000/275000)·8.0 + (25000/275000)·7.0 = 7.91`
  - Mid-vote film: `v=30,000`, `R=8.7` → `WR = (30000/55000)·8.7 + (25000/55000)·7.0 = 7.93`
  - [Computed directly from the formula above.] Note the niche film's astronomically high raw average (9.5) is almost entirely discounted by the huge fixed prior weight — this is intentionally aggressive shrinkage, tuned for a Top-250-style "must have broad, sustained support" use case, not necessarily appropriate for general product comparison.
- **Caveats / false positives:** IMDb explicitly states it does **not** disclose the full anti-manipulation weighting it layers on top of this base formula ("a different weighting calculation may be applied" when abuse is detected) — the published formula is necessarily a simplification of what's actually displayed. Also: widely-repeated online claims that `m≈1,300` or `m=25` are **outdated/incorrect** — verify against the current IMDb Help page before using a specific `m`.

### F7. James–Stein / empirical-Bayes shrinkage
- **Claim:** When estimating many related quantities at once (e.g., every seller's true return rate, every SKU's true defect rate), shrinking each individual estimate toward the *group* average provably reduces total estimation error versus trusting each raw estimate on its own, once you have ≥3 groups.
- **Mechanism:** Stein's paradox: under squared-error loss, the vector of raw per-group sample means is an inadmissible estimator of the vector of true group means whenever there are 3 or more groups (`p≥3`); shrinking each group's estimate toward the grand mean by a data-driven amount reduces the summed expected squared error, even though it can make any *individual* estimate slightly worse.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** Basic form: `estimate_i = x̄ + c·(x_i − x̄)`, where `x̄` is the grand mean across all `i` groups and `c<1` is a shrinkage factor. One standard empirical-Bayes estimate of the shrinkage intensity: `λ* = (p−3) / Σ_k (x_k − x̄)²`, giving shrunk estimate `μ̂_shrink_k = λ*·x̄ + (1−λ*)·x_k` (roughly: the more the individual `x_k` cluster together relative to noise, the more you should shrink each toward the group mean).
- **Strength:** Peer-reviewed, foundational statistics (Stein 1956; James & Stein 1961); empirically validated on real data by Efron & Morris (1977).
- **Source(s):** B. Efron & C. Morris (1977), on shrinkage estimation, discussed with the original baseball dataset in Efron's Stanford course notes, https://efron.ckirby.su.domains/other/CASI_Chap7_Nov2014.pdf; classic worked reproduction: "The Future of Indirect Evidence," https://arxiv.org/pdf/1012.1161 — reports that on Carl Morris's 1970 dataset of 18 players' batting averages (45 at-bats each), predicting the rest of the season, **the James–Stein estimator's total squared prediction error (0.027) was less than one-third of the raw sample means' error (0.086)**.
- **Caveats / false positives:** Requires ≥3 comparable groups being estimated *simultaneously* — it's a "borrow strength across many similar items" technique, not a single-item fix; an agent evaluating one product in isolation cannot apply James–Stein directly, but can apply the *conceptual* lesson: an individual product's raw rating is noisier evidence than it looks, and category-level context should pull extreme small-n scores toward the category norm.

### F8. The J-shaped / bimodal distribution is the normal, honest shape
- **Claim:** Product-review distributions are naturally J-shaped or bimodal (heavy at 5★, a smaller bump at 1★, thin middle) — this shape by itself is *not* evidence of manipulation; it results from two identified, non-fraudulent self-selection mechanisms.
- **Mechanism (from the peer-reviewed model):** (1) **Acquisition/purchasing bias** — people mostly buy products they already expect to like (based on price, brand, description), so the pool of *people who ever try the product* is pre-skewed positive relative to the general population. (2) **Under-reporting ("brag-or-moan") bias** — among people who did try it, those with *extreme* reactions (loved it / hated it) are disproportionately likely to bother writing a review at all, versus people with a lukewarm 3-star reaction.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** No closed-form correction formula is given by these papers (they model and explain the shape, they don't hand you a de-biasing statistic to apply from a product page); qualitatively, expect real, organic review sets to show a J-shape/bimodality, and treat both the height of the 5★ bar *and* a nontrivial 1★ presence as jointly normal, not contradictory.
- **Strength:** Peer-reviewed (MIS Quarterly, top IS journal) plus supporting CACM piece.
- **Source(s):** N. Hu, P.A. Pavlou, J. Zhang (2017), "On Self-Selection Biases in Online Product Reviews," *MIS Quarterly*, 41(2), 449–471, https://misq.umn.edu/misq/article/41/2/449/335/On-Self-Selection-Biases-in-Online-Product ; N. Hu, J. Zhang, P.A. Pavlou (2009), "Overcoming the J-shaped Distribution of Product Reviews," *Communications of the ACM*, 52(10), 144–147, https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1562764.1562800 ; earlier working paper with the "brag-or-moan" phrase spelled out, https://archive.nyu.edu/bitstream/2451/14951/2/usedbook1.pdf
- **Caveats / false positives:** The papers show consumers only *partially* correct for these biases even when trying to (bounded rationality) — so an agent should actively correct for them (e.g., weight the distribution shape, not just the mean) rather than assume readers already do.

### F9. Manipulation signals that *are* quantified — incentivized and purchased fake reviews
- **Claim:** Two credible quantitative studies exist on how much fake/incentivized reviews move visible ratings, and both show the effect is real but the *mean* shift is smaller than intuition suggests — the danger is mostly in *rank/percentile*, and in the fact that the boost is often temporary.
- **Mechanism:** Incentivized reviews (free/discounted product for a review) select for people predisposed to reciprocate positively; purchased reviews on black-market forums are typically front-loaded early in a listing's life, then followed by organic reviews reflecting real (lower) quality once the manipulation is detected/stopped or the product's real flaws surface.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** No single formula; use as calibration numbers. He, Hollenbeck & Proserpio (peer-reviewed, *Marketing Science*) find fake-review purchases produce a **significant but short-term** increase in average rating and review count, which **reverses** after the campaign (or after an Amazon policy shock removed incentivized reviews) — i.e., a rating trajectory that spikes then decays back down over subsequent months is a real, published signal of manipulation, observable by plotting rating-over-time if the platform exposes review dates.
- **Strength:** Peer-reviewed (Marketing Science, top marketing journal) for the He/Hollenbeck/Proserpio study; the "+0.38 stars, 54th→94th percentile" figure below is from an industry (non-peer-reviewed) analysis and should be treated as illustrative, not a hard constant.
- **Source(s):** S. He, B. Hollenbeck, D. Proserpio (2022), "The Market for Fake Reviews," *Marketing Science*, 41(5), 896–921, https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mksc.2022.1353 (documents >20 private Facebook groups averaging 16,000 members each soliciting fake Amazon reviews; estimates reviews purchased for up to 4.5 million products in one year; found buying fake reviews yields a short-term rating/sales bump followed by consumer disappointment). Industry figure (non-academic, use cautiously): Lab 916 analysis of Amazon incentivized reviews, https://www.lab916.com/incentivized-reviews-on-amazon/ (reports incentivized reviews average 4.74★ vs 4.36★ unincentivized, over "7 million Amazon reviews"; original raw dataset/methodology not independently verified here).
- **Caveats / false positives:** The industry figure's underlying dataset and methodology were not verifiable from primary-source text in this pass — treat the "4.74 vs 4.36" and "54th→94th percentile" numbers as **directionally credible but not independently peer-reviewed**; don't quote them as precise constants.

### F10. Benford's law — real for review *text*, not established for star digits
- **Claim:** There is real, published research applying Benford's-law-style digit analysis to online reviews, but it is applied to **word counts of review text**, not to star-rating values, and even there the results are mixed.
- **Mechanism:** Benford's law predicts the distribution of leading digits in naturally-occurring, multi-order-of-magnitude numerical data; deviation can (weakly) flag artificial/generated data.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** None usable for star ratings. The one study found examined **word-count distributions** of review text across Yelp, Amazon, and a Chinese food-delivery platform (Dianping), finding that word counts of review content **do not generally** obey Benford's law overall, though **higher-quality reviews** conform more closely, and conformance is **weaker in emerging markets** than mature ones.
- **Strength:** Peer-reviewed (*Electronic Commerce Research*), but the finding is nuanced/mixed, not a clean detection rule.
- **Source(s):** "A cross-site comparison of online review manipulation using Benford's law," *Electronic Commerce Research* (2021), https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10660-020-09455-8
- **Caveats / false positives:** **No credible source was found supporting Benford's-law analysis of star-rating *values* or review *counts* as a fraud signal** — star ratings only take 1–5 (or similar) integer values, which is exactly the kind of low-cardinality, narrow-range data Benford's law is not designed for (Benford's law requires numbers spanning multiple orders of magnitude). An agent should **not** invent a "Benford check" on a 1–5 star histogram; that would be misapplying the law. State this explicitly as a dead end (also listed below).

### F11. Systematic self-selection in who rates at all — acquisition and under-reporting bias, formalized
- **Claim:** Beyond the shape argument (F8), Hu/Pavlou/Zhang formally show the *mean* rating is a **biased estimator of true product quality**, and quantify (analytically + empirically) that consumers only partially self-correct for it.
- **Mechanism:** Same two biases as F8 (acquisition bias, under-reporting/extreme-response bias), now cast as a formal selection model with testable predictions about how the *distributional parameters* (not just the mean) of a review set relate to actual product quality/future sales.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** No plug-in formula is published; the operational takeaway is to weight variance/skew of the histogram, and the volume of reviews relative to sales/traffic, rather than the mean rating alone, when inferring quality — consistent with Miller's suggestion (F1 source, "Other Applications" section) to prefer "positive ratings per view/purchase" over "positive ratings per rating" when that denominator is observable.
- **Strength:** Peer-reviewed (MIS Quarterly).
- **Source(s):** Same as F8: Hu, Pavlou & Zhang (2017), MIS Quarterly, https://misq.umn.edu/misq/article/41/2/449/335/On-Self-Selection-Biases-in-Online-Product
- **Caveats / false positives:** The "consumers partially correct" finding is about *human* review readers' informal judgment, not about any specific automated correction — it does not itself provide the correction, only evidence that one is needed.

### F12. Social influence / herding — a single early vote measurably inflates the aggregate
- **Claim:** In a large-scale **randomized field experiment**, artificially giving a post/comment a single positive vote (vs. none, vs. a single negative vote) at the moment it appeared caused it to accumulate a significantly higher final score than the untreated control, purely through herding — a causal, not just correlational, effect.
- **Mechanism:** Seeing an existing positive signal makes subsequent raters more likely to also rate positively (informational and normative social influence / conformity); the effect is **asymmetric** — an artificial early positive vote snowballs, but an artificial early negative vote gets self-corrected by later raters ("crowd correction" works for false negatives but not false positives).
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** No formula for retroactive correction; the operational takeaway is that **early ratings on a young listing disproportionately shape everything that follows**, so a rating stream's *early history* (if visible/orderable by date) deserves more scrutiny/skepticism than its later history, and single-digit-n items are especially exposed to this effect.
- **Strength:** Peer-reviewed, top general-science journal, and a true randomized experiment (rare and strong causal evidence in this literature).
- **Source(s):** L. Muchnik, S. Aral, S.J. Taylor (2013), "Social Influence Bias: A Randomized Experiment," *Science*, 341(6146), 647–651, https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1240466 — DOI 10.1126/science.1240466. Reports: a single artificial upvote **increased the likelihood of a subsequent upvote by 32%**, and inflated the post's **final rating by about 25%** through herding; **false-positive** seed votes produced persistent inflation while **false-negative** seed votes had only small long-run effects (crowds partially self-correct downward manipulation but not upward manipulation).
- **Caveats / false positives:** Experiment run on a social-news-aggregation site (Reddit-like), not literally a product-review platform — the underlying herding mechanism is domain-general social psychology and plausibly transfers, but the exact 25%/32% figures are specific to that platform's dynamics, not a universal constant.

### F13. Sequential and anchoring bias within a review stream
- **Claim:** The order in which reviews appear on a page, and the content of the reviews a person reads first, measurably bias what they write next.
- **Mechanism:** Reviews shown first get disproportionate attention (eye-tracking shows reviews later in a list are processed more superficially); a reader who sees an already-"helpful"-voted favorable review anchors toward agreeing with it, while a reader confronted with a critical top review shows a **counter-anchoring** (risk-averse, more skeptical) response instead of pure conformity.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** No numeric formula; operational implication: the platform's *default sort order* for reviews (helpful-first, recent-first, etc.) itself shapes the visible distribution — an agent auditing a rating should note which sort order it's reading under and, where possible, check whether the histogram (all-time aggregate) differs from what the top-sorted reviews alone would suggest.
- **Strength:** Peer-reviewed (Journal of Organizational Computing and Electronic Commerce); related follow-up experimental replication exists (Information & Management, 2014).
- **Source(s):** F. Wu, B.A. Huberman (2009), "Sequential Bias in Online Product Reviews," *Journal of Organizational Computing and Electronic Commerce*, 19(2), https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/10919390902820764 ; experimental replication: "Experimental evaluation of sequential bias in online customer reviews," *Information & Management* (2014), https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S037872061400113X
- **Caveats / false positives:** Full text of the original paper was not directly read in this pass (paywalled); findings above are drawn from consistent secondary summaries across independent citing sources, not from the primary PDF itself — flag as moderately, not maximally, verified.

### F14. Reciprocity and retaliation bias in two-sided marketplaces
- **Claim:** When both parties to a transaction rate each other (host↔guest, buyer↔seller), ratings become **strategically entangled** — fear of retaliation and desire to reciprocate goodwill inflate ratings well above what a one-sided rating system would show, and the true negative-experience rate is systematically hidden.
- **Mechanism / evidence:**
  - **Airbnb ratings are extremely and structurally inflated** relative to comparable one-sided or asymmetric systems: nearly **95% of Airbnb listings have an average rating of 4.5 or 5.0 stars** (the maximum), essentially none below 3.5; by contrast, on TripAdvisor, **hotels average 3.8★ and B&Bs 4.1★, with far more variance**; TripAdvisor's own vacation-rental ratings (same asset type as Airbnb) show only **~85%** at 4.5–5★, still lower than Airbnb's ~95%; **for listings cross-posted on both platforms, ratings correlate only weakly** — pattern held consistently across 2015 and 2018 data (i.e., a structural, not transient, feature).
  - A causal fix was tested: hiding each side's review from the other until **both** submit (removing the ability to retaliate/reciprocate strategically) **increased total reviewing** and **reduced retaliation/reciprocation, leading to *lower* (more honest) ratings** — a randomized field experiment on Airbnb.
  - On eBay, the naive "percent positive" metric hides how much feedback goes **unreported** (only ~65% of transactions get any feedback at all); a corrected metric, "**Effective Percent Positive**," accounts for the missing/unreported share.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:**
  - Cross-platform triangulation: if a two-sided marketplace listing (rental, freelancer, gig worker) sits at ≥4.5/5 with near-zero variance, treat that as **structurally uninformative** rather than exceptional — compare instead to the platform's *own* baseline distribution (per Zervas et al., ~95% of Airbnb listings are already at that level) or to a comparable one-sided-rating platform for the same asset if one exists (e.g., TripAdvisor for a rental).
  - eBay-style "Effective Percent Positive": `EPP = (positive feedback transactions) / (total transactions)`, i.e. divide by **all transactions**, not just the ones that received any feedback. Worked example from the source: two sellers, A (120 transactions, 99 positive, 1 negative, 20 silent/no-feedback) and B (150 transactions, 99 positive, 1 negative, 50 silent): both show naive "percent positive" = 99%, but **EPP_A ≈ 83%** and **EPP_B ≈ 66%** — the seller with more silent (unreported, plausibly worse) transactions looks meaningfully worse once you don't just discard missing data. An agent should ask, wherever a platform shows "X% positive of reviewed transactions," what the *denominator* would look like if it included un-reviewed transactions too.
- **Strength:** Peer-reviewed for all three: Marketing Letters (Zervas et al.), Marketing Science (Fradkin et al.), NBER/AEA-adjacent working paper widely cited and later published (Nosko & Tadelis).
- **Source(s):** G. Zervas, D. Proserpio, J.W. Byers (2021), "A First Look at Online Reputation on Airbnb, Where Every Stay is Above Average," *Marketing Letters*, 32(1), https://open.bu.edu/items/8ec37d60-4f29-4b41-94c9-fe04bbf68885 ; A. Fradkin, E. Grewal, D. Holtz (2021), "Reciprocity and Unveiling in Two-Sided Reputation Systems: Evidence from an Experiment on Airbnb," *Marketing Science*, https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/abs/10.1287/mksc.2021.1311 (SSRN preprint: https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2939064) ; C. Nosko, S. Tadelis, "The Limits of Reputation in Platform Markets: An Empirical Analysis and Field Experiment," NBER Working Paper 20830, https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w20830/w20830.pdf (definition and worked example of Effective Percent Positive at https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/stadelis/EPP.pdf).
- **Caveats / false positives:** The Airbnb/eBay findings are platform-specific to *two-sided* markets where both parties rate each other — this reciprocity mechanism doesn't apply to one-directional product reviews (buyer rates product, product doesn't rate buyer), so don't over-generalize it to, say, Amazon product ratings.

### F15. Rating inflation over time and cross-platform baselines differ — a rating is only meaningful relative to its platform's own norm
- **Claim:** Average ratings on a given platform tend to drift upward over the platform's lifetime and differ systematically by platform, so a "4.5" means different things on different sites and different eras of the same site.
- **Mechanism:** As a platform's own displayed average rating rises (from selection effects, review-solicitation intensity, and normalization — "if everyone else gives 5 stars, giving 4 feels like punishment"), new raters calibrate to what they perceive as normal, reinforcing the drift; more aggressive review solicitation (e.g., Google's Local Guides program) plus laxer moderation independently push the platform average up.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** No single correction formula; the operational rule is: **never compare a raw star rating across platforms without knowing each platform's typical/base rate.** Concretely, per the Google-ratings analysis: the average Google Business rating rose from **3.74★ (Jan 2015) to 4.11★ (Jul 2022)**, roughly a 10% rise over 7 years — so a 4.1★ business in 2015 and a 4.1★ business in 2022 are not equivalent evidence of quality. Separately, on Yelp, a listing's **very first review overestimates its eventual settled rating** — first reviews average **4.1★** while, by the 20th review, the running average has settled to **~3.69★** (the "warm-start bias" of early reviews).
- **Strength:** The Yelp warm-start finding is from an arXiv-hosted empirical analysis (not confirmed peer-reviewed venue in this pass — treat as solid empirical work, moderate-strength citation); the Google ratings-over-time figures are from an industry analytics vendor (SOCi), non-academic — treat as **industry-report strength**, not peer-reviewed.
- **Source(s):** "The warm-start bias of Yelp ratings," https://arxiv.org/pdf/1202.5713 ; SOCi, "The State of Google Reviews," https://www.soci.ai/insights/state-of-google-reviews/ ; corroborating academic pointer (news writeup of Stanford research on rating inflation and word-based vs. star-based systems): Stanford Engineering, "This story is rated five stars!," https://engineering.stanford.edu/news/story-rated-five-stars (summarizing Garg & Johari's peer-reviewed research comparing word-based and star-based rating systems' susceptibility to inflation — reports ~95% of Airbnb hosts and ~80% of freelance-platform workers rated at/near the maximum).
- **Caveats / false positives:** The precise Google percentages are vendor-reported, not independently reproduced; treat the *direction* (platforms inflate over time, and differ from each other) as well-evidenced, and any specific percentage as illustrative.

### F16. Culture/country differences in how people use rating scales
- **Claim:** The same underlying satisfaction level produces measurably different star-rating behavior depending on the rater's cultural background — extreme-response tendency is not culturally universal.
- **Mechanism:** "Extreme response style" (ERS) — the tendency to pick scale endpoints rather than midpoints — correlates with individualism, power distance, and other Hofstede-style cultural dimensions; also depends on the *language* the rating form is presented in (native-language forms elicit more extreme responses than a respondent's non-native language).
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** No numeric correction available for a single rating (individual rater's country of origin usually isn't visible), but at the *aggregate* level: if a platform/product's rater base skews toward a country/culture with documented low-ERS behavior (e.g., Japan), expect systematically more midpoint (3-star-equivalent) ratings even for objectively good products, and vice versa for high-ERS cultures (documented for the US). Reported magnitude: in one classic study, **US respondents picked extreme scale values ~41% more often than Japanese respondents (19.2% vs. 13.6%)**, while Japanese respondents picked the midpoint **~33% more often** (23.2% vs. 17.4%).
- **Strength:** Peer-reviewed, cross-cultural psychology (Chen, Lee & Stevenson 1995 is the classic study; a later 26-country study replicates and extends it).
- **Source(s):** C. Chen, S. Lee, H.W. Stevenson (1995), "Response Style and Cross-Cultural Comparisons of Rating Scales Among East Asian and North American Students," *Psychological Science*, https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1111/j.1467-9280.1995.tb00327.x (also open-access version: https://escholarship.org/uc/item/1b00n182); 26-country replication discussed in "Extreme Response Style," https://www.researchgate.net/publication/316081993_Extreme_Response_Style
- **Caveats / false positives:** These studies use Likert survey scales in an academic/psychometric context, not e-commerce 1–5-star review widgets directly — transfer to online shopping reviews is plausible but not independently confirmed in this literature search; do not treat the 41%/33% figures as literally applicable to any specific product-review platform's rater mix, which is usually unknown/unobservable to an outside agent anyway.

### F17. The review window is early; the failure window is late — a timing mismatch built into most rating systems
- **Claim:** The large majority of reviews are written shortly after purchase/first use, while a meaningful share of real-world quality problems (durability, reliability, long-term support/software decay) only manifest months to years later — so even an unbiased mean rating still systematically **undersamples long-term failure**.
- **Mechanism:** Nothing about "how satisfied was I on day one" logically constrains "will this still work in year three"; unless a platform specifically re-solicits reviews after a long ownership period, its review corpus is a snapshot of first-weeks sentiment, not lifetime reliability.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** No correction formula exists in the literature searched; the credible, source-backed operational answer is: **prefer or seek out data sources that are explicitly designed to measure the multi-year window** rather than trying to infer it from a review-date-agnostic star average. Consumer Reports' own published reliability methodology explicitly targets this: its "Predicted [Brand] Reliability" ratings are "based on a statistical model that estimates problem rates **within the first five years of ownership** (first two years for smartphones)" — i.e., a purpose-built long-horizon instrument, distinct from and complementary to a typical review-site star average.
- **Strength:** Consumer Reports' own methodology page is a primary, dated, named source (**vendor methodology**, but from a non-profit, independence-audited testing organization, historically a much stronger source class than a typical review platform). The general "reviews reflect early-use satisfaction, not durability" framing beyond this specific instrument is supported mainly by non-academic/practitioner sources found in this search and should be treated as weaker.
- **Source(s):** Consumer Reports, "Rating Methods," https://data.consumerreports.org/rating-methods/ ("Data is collected in the CR Member Annual Reliability Survey..."; "Predicted Brand Reliability ratings are based on a statistical model that estimates problem rates within the first five years of ownership (first two years for smartphones)..."); CR Reliability Ratings Methodology Bulletin (PDF), https://data.consumerreports.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/04/Methodological-Bulletin-Reliability-Weights-CR.pdf
- **Caveats / false positives:** The specific "reviews are written in week 1, failures happen in year 2–5" framing with round numbers (30/60/90-day "honeymoon period") traced in this search to non-academic blog content (e.g., a device-review blog), **not** a peer-reviewed or regulator source — usable as an intuitive framing, but any specific day-count claimed for the "honeymoon period" should be flagged `UNSOURCED` / practitioner-anecdotal, not cited as established fact. The Consumer Reports five-year figure, by contrast, is a direct, dated, named methodological claim and can be cited with confidence as *that organization's* chosen window (not as a universal constant for all products).

### F18. Inverse-variance weighting — the principled way to combine heterogeneous evidence
- **Claim:** When combining multiple independent estimates of the same underlying quantity (e.g., a lab-measured spec vs. a user-rating-derived estimate vs. a professional review's verdict), the statistically efficient way to combine them is to weight each by the inverse of its variance, not to average them naively or weight purely by sample size.
- **Mechanism:** This is the standard fixed-effect meta-analysis pooling formula: a source with a smaller sampling variance (i.e., a more precise/reliable measurement) should count for more, and the weighting `w_i=1/v_i` is provably the choice that minimizes the variance (maximizes precision) of the pooled estimate, under the assumption that all sources are estimating the *same* true value and their errors are independent.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** Given several estimates `Y_i` of the same quantity, each with standard error `SE_i` (so variance `v_i = SE_i²`):

  `w_i = 1/v_i`  →  pooled estimate `Ŷ = ( Σ_i w_i·Y_i ) / ( Σ_i w_i )`  →  pooled SE `= √(1/Σ_i w_i)`

- **Strength:** Textbook/peer-reviewed statistical methodology; canonical reference is the Cochrane Handbook (medical meta-analysis), directly transferable math.
- **Source(s):** Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions, Chapter 10 ("Analysing data and undertaking meta-analyses"), https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/handbook/current/chapter-10 ; MedCalc manual, "Meta-analysis: generic inverse variance method," https://www.medcalc.org/en/manual/meta-analysis-generic.php
- **Worked example (lab spec vs. crowd-rating-derived estimate of the same real-world quantity, e.g. "actual battery life in hours"):**
  - Lab measurement (n=5 controlled bench tests): mean = 10.2 h, SE = 0.3 h → variance = 0.09
  - User-rating-derived estimate (large sample, but self-reported/noisy/potentially biased): mean = 9.0 h, SE = 1.5 h → variance = 2.25
  - Weights: `w_lab = 1/0.09 ≈ 11.11`, `w_user = 1/2.25 ≈ 0.44`
  - Pooled estimate: `(11.11×10.2 + 0.44×9.0)/(11.11+0.44) ≈ 10.15 h`, pooled SE ≈ 0.29 h — the small, precise lab measurement dominates the pooled estimate (~96% of the weight) *despite* the user-derived estimate coming from far more individual data points. [Computed directly from the formulas above with the stated illustrative SEs.]
- **Caveats / false positives — the critical one:** Inverse-variance weighting only accounts for **random sampling variance** — it says nothing about **systematic bias** (selection bias, herding, reciprocity inflation, F8–F16). A biased source's SE can shrink arbitrarily with more ratings while its *bias* stays constant; naively inverse-variance-weighting a huge, precise, but structurally biased crowd average will make the pooled estimate confidently wrong. An agent must first ask "is this source's error random or systematic?" — only random-error sources should be weighted this way; systematic bias needs a structural correction (e.g., subtracting an estimated inflation offset, per F14/F15) or exclusion, not down-weighting by variance alone.

### F19. Contradiction between a lab measurement and a crowd rating average — how to reason about it
- **Claim:** There is no single rule for "which wins"; the correct approach (drawn from F18's logic plus the bias-literature above) is to diagnose *why* they disagree before combining or picking a winner.
- **Mechanism / decision procedure (synthesized from the sourced findings above, not itself a single citable study):**
  1. Check whether the lab measurement and the crowd rating are actually measuring the **same construct** (e.g., a lab dB noise measurement vs. a crowd's subjective "quiet enough" rating are related but not identical; disagreement may be a construct mismatch, not an error in either).
  2. If they should agree, check the crowd source for the biases catalogued above (self-selection/J-shape F8, herding F12, reciprocity F14, platform inflation F15, timing mismatch F17) — a documented bias mechanism that would push the crowd rating in the observed direction is grounds to discount it, *not* average it in at face value.
  3. If no specific bias mechanism explains the gap, and both sources have genuinely low variance (large lab n, large and demographically broad crowd n), treat the disagreement itself as informative — it may mean the lab test doesn't reflect real-world use conditions (labs can also be unrepresentative), and inverse-variance-pool only after adjusting each source's stated variance to also reflect its *external validity*, which numerically usually means treating the crowd source's effective variance as much larger than its raw SE suggests (because SE only captures sampling noise, not bias/model error).
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** No single formula; this is a structured reasoning checklist, explicitly derived here (not directly lifted from one paper) from the combination of F8/F12/F14/F15/F18 above.
- **Strength:** Synthesis / reasoned inference from the peer-reviewed findings above — label this finding itself as **agent-derived synthesis**, not a direct citation.
- **Source(s):** Derived from F8, F12, F14, F15, F18 sources above.
- **Caveats / false positives:** This is explicitly the one finding in this file that is a reasoned synthesis rather than a single sourced claim — treat it as a suggested checklist, not an established result, and don't cite it as "research shows."

### F20. Consumer Reports' two-stream model as a real, working exemplar of principled aggregation
- **Claim:** At least one major, long-running, non-profit consumer organization has already operationalized the "combine independent lab testing with large-sample user surveys, don't just average" approach at scale, and publishes (some of) its methodology.
- **Mechanism:** Consumer Reports runs **63 in-house testing labs** performing standardized, repeatable tests (safety, durability, energy efficiency, performance, ease of use) *and separately* surveys **hundreds of thousands of members** each year about real-world ownership experience (the Annual Reliability Survey, run on a rotating schedule by product category); each product's final CR rating **incorporates both streams**, plus a specific "Predicted Reliability" statistical model targeting the multi-year failure window (F17).
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** No single number; the operational lesson for an agent is that a credible aggregator (a) keeps controlled measurement and self-reported experience as **separate, labeled streams** rather than blending them invisibly, and (b) explicitly targets the long-ownership window separately from initial-satisfaction sentiment — a two-source, two-timeframe model an agent can approximate manually by deliberately seeking out an independent test/spec (lab-like) source *and* a large-sample ownership-survey-like source (e.g., long-term-owner forum threads, warranty-claim rate data) rather than relying on one review platform's star average alone.
- **Strength:** Vendor/organizational methodology, but from a source with an unusually strong independence mandate (dues-funded non-profit, no advertising, explicit "free of any corporate or industry influence" claim) — treat as **high-quality vendor methodology**, a different and generally more trustworthy class than a typical for-profit review platform.
- **Source(s):** Consumer Reports, "Product Testing and Research," https://www.consumerreports.org/cro/about-us/what-we-do/research-testing/index.htm ; Consumer Reports, "Rating Methods," https://data.consumerreports.org/rating-methods/
- **Caveats / false positives:** Consumer Reports' full statistical methodology (exact weighting between lab and survey streams, exact reliability model) is only partially public (bulletins summarize but don't fully specify the model); treat this as evidence that principled two-stream aggregation is achievable and precedented, not as a literal formula to copy.

### F21. Survivorship bias in long-lived, currently-listed items
- **Claim:** The set of items/listings/businesses still visible on a platform today is not a random sample of everything that ever existed there — poor performers are disproportionately removed (delisted, closed, discontinued) over time, so a long-tenured item's rating history is drawn from a survivor-filtered population, which structurally inflates the apparent typical quality of "what's out there."
- **Mechanism:** Low ratings are not just an outcome but a *cause* of removal: badly-rated businesses/products lose revenue and are more likely to shut down, get delisted by the platform, or get discontinued by the seller; once gone, their (bad) rating history stops contributing to any snapshot of "current average quality," so any read of currently-active listings is contaminated by the very selection process that separates survivors from failures. This is the general survivorship-bias mechanism (well known from finance: a US equities dataset restricted to currently-listed firms is missing roughly 75% of the firms that were actually trading 10 years ago) applied to the ratings domain.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** No correction formula from a product page alone (the failed comparison set is, by definition, no longer visible). Operationally: (a) do not infer "reviews are reliable because this listing has survived N years with a good rating" without accounting for the fact that surviving N years is itself evidence of selection, not just quality — many similarly-rated newcomers may have already failed and vanished from the comparison set; (b) where a platform or third-party dataset preserves delisted/closed entries (e.g., Yelp's "closed" flag, app-store version histories, archived listings), prefer that survivorship-bias-free view over a live-listings-only search; (c) treat a rating's demonstrated causal link to exit (below) as grounds to expect the *visible* population's average to run higher than the *true historical* population's average, especially in categories with high turnover (restaurants, apps, gig-work listings, crowdfunded products).
- **Strength:** Peer-reviewed / NBER working paper (economics) for the direct empirical causal link between rating and exit; peer-reviewed (Economics Letters) for the closure-correlate-with-low-rating finding; the general survivorship-bias mechanism itself is textbook methodology (most rigorously documented in finance, cited here as the general mechanism, not a ratings-specific claim).
- **Source(s):** D.L. Luca & M. Luca (2018, rev.), "Survival of the Fittest: The Impact of the Minimum Wage on Firm Exit," NBER Working Paper No. 25806 / Harvard Business School Working Paper 17-088, https://www.nber.org/papers/w25806 (full text: https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w25806/w25806.pdf) — finds restaurants with lower Yelp ratings are systematically closer to the margin of exit and disproportionately driven out of business by cost shocks (a $1 minimum-wage increase raised the exit likelihood of a 3.5-star restaurant, the Yelp median, by about 10%, with "no discernible impact" on 5-star restaurants); D. Sedov (2022), "Restaurant closures during the COVID-19 pandemic: A descriptive analysis," *Economics Letters*, 213, https://sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0165176522000593 — using Yelp + SafeGraph establishment data, finds lower-rated restaurants were significantly more likely to close during 2020.
- **Caveats / false positives:** These two studies are restaurant-specific (Yelp data); the *general* survivorship-bias mechanism is domain-general statistical logic, but its magnitude for other verticals (SaaS tools, consumer electronics, contractors, supplements) was not independently quantified in this search — treat the restaurant-exit numbers as an illustrative, sourced instance of the mechanism, not a universal effect size. Also note a confound: minimum-wage-driven exit (Luca & Luca's actual research question) is one specific *cause* of exit; the finding used here is the auxiliary, but directly stated, fact that exit risk correlates strongly with rating in their data, not the paper's main causal claim about wages.

## Operational checklist candidates

1. Before trusting any star average, get the **n** and, if available, the **5-bucket histogram** — never rank or trust a bare mean (F1).
2. For binary (thumbs/helpful) signals, compute the **Wilson score lower bound** from (positive, n) using the formula in F2, at z=1.96, rather than the raw percentage.
3. For 5-star histograms, compute the **Dirichlet-multinomial credible lower bound** (F3) from the full histogram, not the mean; treat two items' rankings as reversed if their lower bounds are reversed relative to their raw means (this is expected and correct, not an error).
4. When a product/listing has a very small n and a near-perfect score, **shrink it toward the category baseline** using additive smoothing or an informative Beta prior (F5) before comparing it to well-established competitors.
5. Do **not** treat a J-shaped (mostly-5★, some-1★, thin-middle) histogram as inherently suspicious — it is the documented normal shape of honest review data (F8). Look instead for an **unusually smooth, over-concentrated, or all-perfect** histogram with a documented anomaly (below).
6. Do treat a rating trajectory that **spikes then decays** over time (visible if the platform timestamps reviews) as a real, published signal of a fake-review campaign followed by organic correction (F9).
7. Do **not** run a Benford's-law digit check on star-rating values or review counts — no credible source supports this, and star ratings' narrow 1–5 range makes Benford's law inapplicable by construction (F10).
8. Never compare raw star averages **across platforms** without accounting for each platform's own baseline/inflation level (F15) — a 4.5★ is not the same evidence on every site or in every year.
9. For two-sided marketplaces (rentals, gig work, freelancers), treat near-universal 4.5–5★ clustering as **structurally uninformative low-variance noise**, and where the platform exposes it, prefer an "effective percent positive"-style metric that divides by **all** transactions, not just reviewed ones (F14).
10. Explicitly discount early reviews and prefer sources built for the multi-year ownership window (Consumer Reports-style reliability data, warranty-claim data, long-term-owner forum threads) when the question is durability/reliability rather than initial impression (F17, F20).
11. When combining a lab/spec measurement with a crowd-rating-derived estimate, use **inverse-variance weighting** (F18) — but first separate random noise (which shrinks with n and can be weighted) from systematic bias (which does not shrink with n and must instead be diagnosed and corrected or excluded, F19).
12. If asked to give a single verdict from contradictory lab and crowd evidence, run the diagnostic checklist in F19 rather than silently averaging or silently picking one source.
13. When judging a long-tenured, well-rated listing, remember its long tenure is itself evidence of survivorship filtering, not proof the "average" competitor is that good — where possible, check for a delisted/closed-inclusive dataset rather than only the live listings (F21).

## Dead ends and myths

- **"A 5.0/5.0 average is the best possible signal of quality."** Not supported — a perfect small-n average is exactly the case the entire literature above (F1–F7) says to be *most* skeptical of; in two-sided marketplaces, near-5★ is the *typical*, uninformative outcome for ~95% of listings, not a distinguishing signal (F14, Zervas/Proserpio/Byers).
- **"Just use the Wilson score formula directly on a 5-star average."** Wrong application — Wilson's formula is derived for **binary** Bernoulli data; a 5-star mean is not a proportion. The correct analogue is the Dirichlet-multinomial credible interval (F3). No source supports plugging a star-rating percentage into the binary Wilson formula.
- **"A specific 5★:4★ ratio (e.g., 3:1, 5:1) or a specific 1-star percentage threshold reliably flags fake reviews."** **No peer-reviewed or regulator source found supporting any specific numeric ratio/threshold.** The J-shape/self-selection literature (F8) supports the *qualitative* claim that some middle-rating mass and some 1-star presence is normal; industry blog content proposes specific numeric "red flag" ratios, but none of it traces to a verifiable dataset or peer-reviewed methodology in this search. Treat any specific ratio threshold as **unsourced folklore**.
- **"Benford's law can be applied to star-rating digits or review counts to catch manipulation."** Not supported for star values (F10) — the one relevant peer-reviewed study applies Benford's law to review **word counts**, not ratings, and even there finds mixed conformance. Star ratings (1–5 integers) are the wrong data type for Benford's law by construction (no multi-order-of-magnitude spread).
- **"IMDb's minimum-vote parameter `m` is 1,300 (or 25)."** Outdated/incorrect — as of the IMDb Help page dated Feb 9, 2026 (the primary, official, current source), `m = 25,000`. Older explainer posts citing 1,300 or 25 reflect stale or unverified figures; always check the live IMDb Help page rather than citing a remembered constant (F6).
- **"More ratings always means more reliable evidence, full stop."** Partially true but incomplete and dangerous if taken literally — more ratings shrink **random** sampling variance (correctly captured by Wilson/Dirichlet lower bounds, F2/F3), but do **not** shrink **systematic** bias from herding (F12), reciprocity (F14), platform-level inflation (F15), or timing mismatch (F17). A huge-n rating can be huge-n *and* systematically wrong.
- **"The honeymoon period for a product review is exactly 30/60/90 days."** These specific day-counts trace in this search only to non-academic practitioner blog content, not to a peer-reviewed or regulator study — usable as an intuitive heuristic, but should be flagged `UNSOURCED` if presented as an established figure. The durable, sourced claim is the qualitative one plus Consumer Reports' explicit multi-year (up to 5-year) reliability-survey window (F17).

## Open questions

- No source found gives a general, cross-category numeric answer to "how many ratings is *enough*" — F3's derivation shows the answer depends heavily on whether ratings already agree (linear scaling with target precision) or are spread out (quadratic scaling), which an agent can estimate from the observed histogram but which has no single universal number.
- The magnitude of incentivized-review rating inflation (the "+0.38 stars, 54th→94th percentile" figures in F9) rests on an industry (non-peer-reviewed) analysis; a rigorous, independently reproducible peer-reviewed effect size for *incentivized* (as distinct from outright fake/purchased) reviews specifically was not located in this pass.
- Whether platform-internal fraud-detection systems (which see reviewer network structure, IP/device signals, and purchase-verification data unavailable to an outside agent) achieve meaningfully better accuracy than the purely rating-distribution-based heuristics catalogued here is unknown from public sources — Amazon's own trust page (https://trustworthyshopping.aboutamazon.com/how-amazon-maintains-a-trusted-review-experience) references such internal signals but does not publish validation figures.
- No study located directly tests whether an *informative* (empirically J-shaped) Dirichlet prior, rather than the flat/uniform prior used in F3's derivation, would produce a meaningfully tighter or more accurate credible interval for real review data — this seems like a natural extension of Evan Miller's method that nobody in this search has published.
- The cross-cultural rating-scale-use findings (F16) come from psychometric survey literature, not from e-commerce review platforms directly — whether the same magnitude of difference (e.g., the 41%/33% figures) replicates in actual 1–5-star product-review behavior specifically remains an open, untested transfer.

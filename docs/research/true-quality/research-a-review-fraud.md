# Fake, paid, incentivised and manipulated reviews — detection signals and platform mechanics — research findings

## Executive summary

- Review-fraud detection has a 15+ year academic literature (opinion-spam mining) that converges on a small set of *behavioral* and *network* signals — burstiness, singleton reviewers, reviewer-product graph density, duplicate/near-duplicate text — that are stronger than any purely linguistic signal, and all of them are things an agent can approximate from a public product page by reading the reviewer's public profile and the review timeline, not just the review text.
- The single most load-bearing empirical fact for this whole skill: online rating distributions are **not** unbiased samples of experience. Self-selection produces a **J-shaped distribution** (mostly 5-star, a spike of 1-star, almost nothing in the middle) even when *zero* reviews are fake (Hu, Pavlou & Zhang 2009/2017). This means "5.0 average" is not intrinsically suspicious by itself — sample size and distribution *shape* matter far more than the mean.
- Star averages are weakly correlated with objective, independently-measured quality: de Langhe, Fernbach & Lichtenstein (2016, *Journal of Consumer Research*) found online average ratings do not converge with Consumer Reports scores across 1,272 products, are driven by insufficient sample sizes, and are inflated for expensive/premium-brand items independent of measured quality. Consumers over-trust the mean; the mean is a weak quality signal on its own.
- Fake reviews are not evenly distributed by motive — Luca & Zervas (2016) show restaurants fake reviews **when reputation is weak** (new or recently damaged), and He, Hollenbeck & Proserpio (2022) show it's not just weak sellers: many high-rated Amazon products *also* buy reviews, so "already has thousands of good reviews" is not itself exculpatory.
- Regulation now creates *forced, observable artifacts* an agent can check directly: the US FTC's 16 CFR Part 465 (effective Oct 21, 2024) bans buying/selling reviews and undisclosed insider reviews; the UK's DMCC Act 2024 (in force April 2025) does the same via a strict-liability "blacklist" entry; the EU's Omnibus Directive (2019/2161) requires platforms to disclose *whether and how* they verify that reviews come from real buyers (UCPD Art. 7). An agent can and should look for these specific disclosure statements — their absence is itself informative post-2024/2025.
- Every major platform now runs *some* form of automated fake-review detection and publishes numbers: Trustpilot removed 4.5M fake reviews in 2024 (7% of all reviews posted that year, 90% caught pre-publication by ML), Google says it blocked/removed 240M+ policy-violating reviews in 2024 and 292M+ in 2025 (~22% of submitted volume), Amazon says it blocked 275M+ suspected fake reviews in 2024. These numbers establish base rates: expect single-digit-to-~20% fake-review contamination as the *platform-reported floor*, not the ceiling — independent academic audits (Yelp: ~16% of restaurant reviews filtered per Luca & Zervas; Norway Consumer Council: up to 16% of hotel reviews showing inauthenticity indicators) land in a similar 10–20% band.
- Verification badges are necessary but not sufficient. "Verified Purchase" on Amazon is trivially gamed by brushing (sending unordered packages to real addresses under real names, producing a bona fide "verified" transaction with zero relationship to genuine use); Booking.com's "only guests who booked can review" is gameable via fake cash-marked bookings that are never fulfilled. An agent must treat "verified" as raising the cost of faking, not eliminating it.
- The consumer-facing free "paste a URL, get a trust score" tools (Fakespot, ReviewMeta, TheReviewIndex) are **all dead or unreliable as of 2026** — Fakespot shut down entirely July 1, 2025 (acquired by Mozilla in 2023, wound down May 2025); ReviewMeta went offline in early 2026 with no official statement; TheReviewIndex shut down citing Amazon policy changes. **The skill must never tell an agent to "check Fakespot/ReviewMeta"** — those instructions will fail silently against a dead site.
- LLM-generated reviews are the frontier problem circa 2024–2026: they are fluent, no longer have the tell-tale spelling/grammar noise of older fake reviews, and multiple 2025–2026 studies report they're near-indistinguishable to both humans and off-the-shelf LLM classifiers in blind tests, while purpose-built detectors claim 90%+ accuracy against known LLM outputs but degrade against unknown generators. Practical implication: linguistic-only detection is now the *weakest* leg of the stool; behavioral/graph/temporal signals matter more, not less.
- B2B software review sites (G2, Capterra, TrustRadius) run *disclosed* incentive programs (small gift cards/donations, capped, disclosure required, review must not be contingent on sentiment) that are legal under FTC guidance but create a different failure mode: response bias toward people willing to write a review for $25–$100, which skews toward casual/light users, not toward power users or churned customers.
- GitHub stars and npm/PyPI download counts are now demonstrably farmable at scale: a 2025/2026 peer-reviewed paper (He et al., "Six Million (Suspected) Fake Stars on GitHub," ICSE 2026) found ~6M suspicious stars across 18,600+ repos using an open, reusable detection method (StarScout) validated against GitHub's own subsequent takedowns (90%+ of flagged repos were later deleted by GitHub itself); npm download counts can be trivially inflated ("download pumping") by an individual with a laptop in about an hour.

## Findings

### F1. Singleton reviewers and duplicate/near-duplicate text are the founding behavioral signals of opinion-spam research
- **Claim:** Jindal & Liu (2008) established that (a) the large majority of reviewers on a platform write exactly one review ("singletons") — 68% wrote only one review, only 8% wrote 5+ — and (b) duplicate or near-duplicate review text across different reviewer accounts is a strong positive-training signal for spam, because genuine reviewers essentially never copy another reviewer's wording.
- **Mechanism:** Spam operations are cheapest to run via disposable, one-shot accounts; templated or lightly-varied text is cheaper to produce at scale than genuinely distinct prose per account.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** On a product/listing page, sample several reviews' text and run a rough near-duplicate check (shared uncommon phrases, near-identical sentence structure) across reviews on the *same* listing and, if reviewer profiles are visible, across *other* listings by the same or co-occurring reviewers. A cluster of reviews sharing distinctive phrasing is a stronger tell than any single review's tone.
- **Strength:** Peer-reviewed (WSDM 2008), foundational, widely replicated.
- **Source(s):** Jindal & Liu, "Opinion Spam and Analysis," WSDM 2008, https://www.cs.uic.edu/~liub/FBS/opinion-spam-WSDM-08.pdf (Feb 2008).
- **Caveats / false positives:** Singleton reviewers are also just... most real reviewers (people who buy one thing and review it once); singleton status alone is very weak evidence. Later work (Sandulescu & Ester 2016) built dedicated singleton-spam detectors precisely because behavioral-history methods can't touch this majority class — an agent has no login-history access and cannot replicate this signal well from a public page alone.

### F2. Deceptive reviews skew "imaginative," genuine reviews skew "informative/concrete" (LIWC linguistic signal)
- **Claim:** Ott, Choi, Cardie & Hancock (2011) built a gold-standard deceptive-vs-truthful hotel review dataset (paying people to write fake reviews vs. real TripAdvisor reviews) and found a bigram+LIWC (Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count) linear SVM classifier hit ~90% accuracy; deceptive reviews use more storytelling/narrative and personal-experience language (imaginative writing), while truthful reviews use more concrete nouns and spatial details tied to the physical environment (informative writing).
- **Mechanism:** People fabricating an experience they didn't have default to narrative/emotional framing because they lack the sensory specifics a genuine visit/use would generate.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** Read the review body; genuine reviews tend to reference specific, checkable, product-mechanical details (exact part names, specific failure modes, comparisons to a specific prior model); fabricated reviews tend toward generic superlatives and narrative arcs ("I was skeptical but then...").
- **Strength:** Peer-reviewed (ACL 2011), highly cited, but built on a *paid-crowdworker* deception dataset, not in-the-wild fraud — external validity to real fraud rings is contested (see caveat).
- **Source(s):** Ott, Choi, Cardie & Hancock, "Finding Deceptive Opinion Spam by Any Stretch of the Imagination," ACL-HLT 2011, https://aclanthology.org/P11-1032/ ; arXiv version https://arxiv.org/abs/1107.4557 (2011).
- **Caveats / false positives:** This signal is the *weakest* leg as of 2024–2026: LLM-generated fake reviews are fluent and can be prompted to include concrete/informative detail, largely erasing the imaginative/informative gap (see F8). Do not weight linguistic style heavily as a standalone signal.

### F3. Yelp's own filter correlates with behavioral features (frequency, account recency, geography, % positive), not language
- **Claim:** Mukherjee, Venkataraman, Liu & Glance (ICWSM 2013) reverse-engineered Yelp's filtering by comparing filtered vs. recommended reviews and found the filter's output correlates with behavioral features — reviewing frequency, account age/recency, geographic clustering of the reviewer's other reviews, and the proportion of reviews that are extremely positive — more than with review text/language features.
- **Mechanism:** Yelp's filter is a trade secret, but its output is consistent with a behavioral/graph model over reviewer accounts rather than a pure text classifier.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** On a Yelp business page, click through to a reviewer's profile: new account, review history clustered in one city/time window, or a review history that's almost entirely 5-star are the observable proxies for the behavioral features the paper identified.
- **Strength:** Peer-reviewed (AAAI ICWSM 2013), reverse-engineering study (not ground truth — Yelp never confirmed the mechanism).
- **Source(s):** Mukherjee, Venkataraman, Liu & Glance, "What Yelp Fake Review Filter Might Be Doing?", ICWSM 2013, https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/ICWSM/article/view/14389 (2013).
- **Caveats / false positives:** Findings describe Yelp's filter as of ~2013; Yelp's actual algorithm has changed materially since (see F17 for the current official description).

### F4. Fake-review incentive is strongest when a business's reputation is weak (new, or recently damaged)
- **Claim:** Luca & Zervas (2016, *Management Science*) found ~16% of Yelp restaurant reviews were filtered as suspicious; a restaurant is significantly more likely to buy/solicit fake reviews when it has few reviews or recently received bad ones; chain restaurants (who benefit less from Yelp reputation) fake less; increased local competition raises the odds a restaurant becomes the *target* of fake *negative* reviews from a rival.
- **Mechanism:** Fake-positive reviews are a cheap way to overcome a cold start or repair damage; fake-negative reviews are a cheap way to sabotage a competitor — both are rational under a review-driven demand model.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** Check listing age and review-count trajectory (if the platform shows a review-count-over-time or date-stamped list): a low-review-count business, or one with a recent negative event followed by a sudden wave of very positive reviews, matches the paper's predicted pattern. Likewise, a business embedded in a dense local competitive cluster (many similar businesses nearby) with an outlier bad rating relative to visible review content is consistent with the sabotage finding.
- **Strength:** Peer-reviewed (Management Science 2016), large-N, causal identification via a natural experiment (Yelp's own sting).
- **Source(s):** Luca & Zervas, "Fake It Till You Make It: Reputation, Competition, and Yelp Review Fraud," Management Science 62(12), Dec 2016, pp. 3412–3427, https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2015.2304 ; working paper https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=45151 (2016).
- **Caveats / false positives:** Restaurant/local-service specific; "new business" is also just... a new legitimate business. Do not treat low review count alone as fraud evidence, only as raising prior probability worth checking further.

### F5. Collective/graph-based detection (network + text + metadata jointly) outperforms any single-signal classifier
- **Claim:** Rayana & Akoglu's SpEagle (KDD 2015) combines review text, metadata (timestamp, rating) and the reviewer–product bipartite graph in one framework, and — evaluated on real Yelp filtered/recommended review data at the largest scale tested to date for this problem — significantly outperforms baselines using only one signal type.
- **Mechanism:** Fraud rings leave traces across multiple channels simultaneously (a burst of reviews, from new accounts, that all touch a narrow set of products, with similar timing) — combining channels catches coordination that no single channel reveals alone.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** Approximate the "graph" signal by checking whether a handful of reviewers on the target listing also reviewed a suspiciously narrow, unrelated set of *other* products in the same short time window (visible via clicking through to reviewer profiles on platforms that expose reviewer history, e.g., Amazon, Yelp, Google Maps "Local Guide" profiles).
- **Strength:** Peer-reviewed (ACM SIGKDD 2015), semi-supervised, largest-scale published eval at the time.
- **Source(s):** Rayana & Akoglu, "Collective Opinion Spam Detection: Bridging Review Networks and Metadata," KDD 2015, https://doi.org/10.1145/2783258.2783370 ; author page https://shebuti.com/collective-opinion-spam-detection/ (2015).
- **Caveats / false positives:** Requires reviewer-level browsing history that not all platforms expose publicly (Amazon does via reviewer profile pages; many platforms don't).

### F6. Temporal burstiness — arrival-rate spikes correlated with rating direction — is a strong unsupervised signal
- **Claim:** Xie, Wang, Lin & Yu (KDD 2012) observed that genuine reviewers' arrival pattern over time is stable and *uncorrelated* with the rating they give, whereas spam campaigns produce *bursts* — short time windows with an unusually high review-arrival rate that is also correlated (positively or negatively) with rating — and built an unsupervised detector purely from these multidimensional time-series correlations, without needing labeled spam examples.
- **Mechanism:** Coordinated campaigns are commissioned and executed in a compressed time window (a buyer pays for N reviews "this week"), which is structurally different from organic demand-driven review arrival.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** If the platform exposes review dates (most do), plot/eyeball review counts per week/month. A sharp spike — especially one skewed heavily toward one star value — clustered in a narrow window is the operational signature; compare against the product's overall sales/visibility trend if inferable (e.g., did the spike follow a major press mention, which would be organic, or not).
- **Strength:** Peer-reviewed (ACM SIGKDD 2012), unsupervised (works without labeled training data, a practical advantage).
- **Source(s):** Xie, Wang, Lin & Yu, "Review Spam Detection via Temporal Pattern Discovery," KDD 2012, https://doi.org/10.1145/2339530.2339662 ; PDF https://www.cs.uic.edu/~slin/assets/Xie_KDD2012.pdf (2012).
- **Caveats / false positives:** Legitimate bursts happen too — a viral launch, a Black Friday sale, a press feature, or (for negative bursts) a genuine safety recall. Burstiness must be read together with rating direction and the review content, not treated as fraud in isolation. Distinguish from "review bombing," a related but distinct phenomenon of coordinated *ideologically motivated* genuine-but-off-topic reviews (see F9-myths/Dead Ends and the review-bombing academic literature at https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.06306 and https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11135-024-01981-z, 2024).

### F7. Fake reviews are commissioned openly in private social-media marketplaces, and buying them measurably moves sales
- **Claim:** He, Hollenbeck & Proserpio (2022, *Marketing Science*) hand-collected data from private Facebook groups (and similar) selling fake Amazon reviews, matched sellers to real Amazon listings, and found: (1) a wide range of products buy fake reviews, *including* products that already have many reviews and high average ratings — not just weak/new sellers; (2) buying fake reviews produces a significant but short-lived bump in rating and review count; (3) exploiting a temporary Amazon policy change, they show rating manipulation has a causally large effect on sales.
- **Mechanism:** Reviews are purchased as a targeted, short-campaign service via organized private groups (see F12 mechanics); the effect decays because Amazon's detection eventually removes many of the fake reviews.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** A visible *rating-count* or *rating-average* discontinuity at a specific date (a jump not explained by a product update, press coverage, or a sale event) is the tell; cross-reference against review dates if the platform allows sorting/filtering by date.
- **Strength:** Peer-reviewed (Marketing Science 2022), causal identification, hand-collected primary market data — one of the strongest sourced findings in this whole corpus.
- **Source(s):** He, Hollenbeck & Proserpio, "The Market for Fake Reviews," Marketing Science 41(5), 2022, pp. 896–921, https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mksc.2022.1353 ; open working paper https://www.anderson.ucla.edu/sites/default/files/document/2025-08/The%20Market%20for%20Fake%20Reviews.pdf (2022).
- **Caveats / false positives:** Critical nuance the skill must preserve: **high volume + high average rating does NOT rule out fake-review purchase.** Do not treat "thousands of good reviews" as automatically clean.

### F8. LLM-generated fake reviews are fluent and, in blind tests, near-indistinguishable from genuine ones; purpose-built detectors work only against known generators
- **Claim:** Multiple 2024–2026 studies converge on: (a) AI-generated fake reviews are now cheap to produce at scale and grammatically/contextually plausible, unlike earlier fake reviews; (b) a 2025 study ("Large Language Models as 'Hidden Persuaders'") found LLM-written fake product reviews were largely indistinguishable to both human raters and off-the-shelf LLM judges; (c) purpose-built detectors combining language + behavioral cues report ~93% (Amazon) / ~91% (Yelp) accuracy against tested corpora, but detection is reported to degrade when the generating LLM is unknown to the detector; (d) platforms report scale growth — Tripadvisor removed 2.7M fake reviews with 214,000 specifically flagged as AI-generated (figure reported by a secondary source, not a Tripadvisor primary transparency report — treat as UNSOURCED-primary), and Yelp reported fraudsters using AI to build fake reviewer personas across many categories to farm "Elite" status before posting fraudulent reviews with algorithmically-boosted weight.
- **Mechanism:** LLMs remove the two weakest historical tells (grammar/spelling errors, and the imaginative-vs-informative language gap from F2) because they can be prompted for concrete, specific, "informative"-sounding detail.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** Linguistic fluency alone is no longer informative. Fall back to behavioral/graph/temporal signals (F1, F5, F6) which do not depend on text style. Watch specifically for near-identical *structure* (not wording) across reviews — LLM-generated batches from the same prompt template often share paragraph structure/argument order even when word choice is varied by the generator.
- **Strength:** Mixed — some peer-reviewed (MDPI journal, ScienceDirect), some arXiv preprints (not yet peer-reviewed), one press-covered but not independently verified accuracy claim (techxplore.com summarizing a study). Treat headline accuracy numbers (93%/91%) as industry-report-strength, not settled science.
- **Source(s):** "Large Language Models as 'Hidden Persuaders'..." arXiv:2506.13313, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.13313 (2025); "Explainable Deep Learning Model for ChatGPT-Rephrased Fake Review Detection Using DistilBERT," MDPI Big Data and Cognitive Computing, https://www.mdpi.com/2504-2289/9/8/205 (Aug 2025); "AI vs. human: A large-scale analysis of AI-generated fake reviews..." ScienceDirect, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0969698925001791 (Jun 2025); techxplore summary of a 93%/91% accuracy claim, https://techxplore.com/news/2026-05-ai-fake-accuracy-amazon-yelp.html (2026, secondary source — underlying paper not independently confirmed in this research pass).
- **Caveats / false positives:** This is the fastest-moving part of the corpus; treat any specific accuracy percentage as provisional and re-verify before relying on it operationally.

### F9. Verified-purchase reviews rate systematically higher than unverified ones — partly genuine signal, partly exploitable
- **Claim:** Multiple sources converge that verified-purchase reviewers rate higher on average than unverified/anonymous reviewers, and that verification measurably increases trust and conversion; an academic self-selection framework (Askalidis, Kim & Malthouse 2017, *Decision Support Systems*) formalizes this as acquisition bias (only people who actually wanted the product buy it) plus under-reporting bias.
- **Mechanism:** Genuine self-selection (buyers already screened for fit before purchase) is the dominant legitimate driver; but verification is also gameable (see F12 brushing) so "verified" raises confidence without eliminating fraud risk.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** Where a platform separately reports verified vs. unverified review counts/ratings (Amazon does), a large gap between verified and unverified averages, or a listing that is *mostly* unverified reviews, is worth flagging; but do not treat "100% verified" as proof of authenticity given F12.
- **Strength:** Academic (Decision Support Systems, peer-reviewed) for the bias framework; industry-report strength (Spiegel Research Center at Northwestern, using PowerReviews data) for the magnitude claims; several cited "amzadvisers/reviewsell"-style blog sources are marketing content, not evidence — excluded from strength claims here.
- **Source(s):** Askalidis, Kim & Malthouse, "Understanding and Overcoming Biases in Customer Reviews," Decision Support Systems, cited via https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/biases-online-product-reviews-what-consumers-managers-need- (2017, secondary summary — locate primary DOI before final citation in skill); Spiegel Research Center, "How Online Reviews Influence Sales," Northwestern University Medill, https://spiegel.medill.northwestern.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2021/04/Spiegel_Online-Review_eBook_Jun2017_FINAL.pdf (2017); Aral & Karahalios-adjacent tablet-market study: "Revealed preference in online reviews: Purchase verification in the tablet market," ScienceDirect, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167923620300361 (2020).
- **Caveats / false positives:** The Askalidis et al. citation above is sourced via a secondary summary (LinkedIn post quoting the paper); the underlying DSS article should be located directly (search "Askalidis Kim Malthouse Decision Support Systems 2017") before the skill treats the specific percentage claims as final. Flagged here as a gap for the writing phase, not dropped.

### F10. Rating distributions are structurally J-shaped even absent any fraud (acquisition bias + under-reporting bias)
- **Claim:** Hu, Pavlou & Zhang (MIS Quarterly 2017, building on 2009 CACM/working paper work) show almost all products on Amazon exhibit an asymmetric bimodal ("J-shaped") distribution — mostly 5-star, a secondary spike at 1-star, very little in between — driven by two *legitimate* self-selection mechanisms: (1) acquisition bias — people who buy a product already had a favorable predisposition toward it, so buyers skew positive before they even use it; (2) under-reporting bias — people with moderate/lukewarm experiences don't bother writing a review; only people who loved it or hated it ("brag or moan") do. A controlled experiment where *all* participants were required to review (removing self-selection) produced an approximately normal distribution instead.
- **Mechanism:** Self-selection at both the purchase stage and the review-writing stage, independent of any manipulation.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** A J-shaped distribution (visible wherever the platform shows a star-by-star histogram, e.g. Amazon, Google, most review platforms) is the *expected baseline shape*, not evidence of fraud by itself. What *is* anomalous: a distribution with the 1-star spike **missing** (near-zero 2–4-star AND near-zero 1-star, i.e. almost pure 5-star with no complaint tail at all) — that pattern is harder to produce organically and is more consistent with review purchase or gating (see F16 review-gating mechanism) than the normal J-shape.
- **Strength:** Peer-reviewed (MIS Quarterly, a top-tier IS journal), econometric + experimental (both secondary-data and controlled-experiment evidence).
- **Source(s):** Hu, Pavlou & Zhang, "On Self-Selection Biases in Online Product Reviews," MIS Quarterly 41(2), 2017, https://aisel.aisnet.org/misq/vol41/iss2/8/ ; earlier CACM summary "Overcoming the J-Shaped Distribution of Product Reviews," https://cacm.acm.org/research/overcoming-the-j-shaped-distribution-of-product-reviews/ (2009/2017).
- **Caveats / false positives:** This is the single most important finding for killing the "5.0 average = something's wrong" naive heuristic (see Dead Ends) — but also means the skill must teach a *more precise* replacement heuristic (missing complaint tail, not just "too high"), not simply "high rating = suspicious."

### F11. Average star ratings correlate weakly with independently-measured (Consumer Reports) product quality
- **Claim:** de Langhe, Fernbach & Lichtenstein (2016, *Journal of Consumer Research*), analyzing 1,272 products across 120 categories, found average online user ratings (1) do not converge with Consumer Reports quality scores, (2) are frequently based on too few reviews to be statistically informative, (3) do not predict resale price in used-goods markets, and (4) are systematically higher for expensive products and premium brands even after controlling for measured (Consumer Reports) quality — while consumers nonetheless treat the star average as highly diagnostic of quality.
- **Mechanism:** Star ratings measure satisfaction-relative-to-expectations and price-adjusted contentment, not absolute engineered quality; premium pricing itself shifts expectations and post-purchase rationalization.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** Do not use the raw star average as a quality estimate for the purpose this skill exists for; instead, treat it as one input alongside review *count*, review *content specifics*, and (where obtainable) independent test-lab or expert-review data. Explicitly downweight star average when comparing a premium/branded item to a cheaper alternative — brand halo inflates ratings independent of quality per this study.
- **Strength:** Peer-reviewed (JCR, top marketing/consumer-behavior journal), large-N.
- **Source(s):** de Langhe, Fernbach & Lichtenstein, "Navigating by the Stars: Investigating the Actual and Perceived Validity of Online User Ratings," Journal of Consumer Research 42(6), 2016, pp. 817–833, https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/42/6/817/2357678 ; PDF https://www.colorado.edu/business/sites/default/files/attached-files/jcr_2016_de_langhe_fernbach_lichtenstein_0.pdf (2016). See also a published commentary contesting the framing/effect size: Winer, "Objective vs. online ratings: are low correlations unexpected and does it matter?", https://www.econbiz.de/Record/objective-vs-online-ratings-are-low-correlations-unexpected-and-does-it-matter-a-commentary-on-de-langhe-fernbach-and-lichtenstein-winer-russell/10011489780 — flag the debate, don't oversell the original finding as undisputed.

### F12. Amazon "brushing" converts an unsolicited, unwanted shipment into a genuine "Verified Purchase" fake review
- **Claim:** Brushing schemes have a scammer create fake buyer accounts, place real (paid, low-cost) orders for a target listing shipped to real people's addresses (often obtained from leaked data) without the recipient's consent or knowledge, then use the same fake account to post a 5-star review — which Amazon labels "Verified Purchase" because a real transaction genuinely occurred.
- **Mechanism:** The verification check (did a real, completed transaction happen) is satisfied even though the "buyer" never chose, wanted, or evaluated the product — the review is fake despite passing verification.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** No direct page-level signal distinguishes a brushed "Verified Purchase" from a genuine one — this is precisely why "Verified Purchase" cannot be treated as sufficient evidence of authenticity, only as raising the cost of fraud relative to unverified reviews. Cross-reference against F1/F5/F6 behavioral signals on the reviewer's profile.
- **Strength:** Regulator/consumer-protection-agency sourced (BBB, Amazon's own customer service pages) plus multiple journalistic explainers; not an academic study.
- **Source(s):** Amazon, "Report Unsolicited Packages or Brushing Scams," https://www.amazon.com/gp/help/customer/display.html?nodeId=G33XVXQPUV79Z2ZC (current); Better Business Bureau, "Brushing scam indicates a serious problem for victims," https://www.bbb.org/article/news-releases/20509-amazon-brushing-scam-indicates-a-serious-problem-for-victims (Aug 2024); Malwarebytes explainer, https://www.malwarebytes.com/cybersecurity/basics/brushing-scam (Oct 2025).
- **Caveats / false positives:** Most brushing-scam explainers are consumer-safety journalism (Aura, LifeLock, McAfee, mail.com), not primary sources — cited above only where the underlying mechanism claim is corroborated by Amazon's own help page and the BBB.

### F13. Amazon Vine is disclosed by mandatory badge; it is a controlled program, but still shifts early-rating dynamics
- **Claim:** Amazon's Vine program gives vetted reviewers ("Vine Voices," selected by Amazon, not paid by the seller) free products in exchange for a review; every Vine review carries a mandatory, non-removable "Vine Customer Review of a Free Product" badge — sellers cannot opt out of the disclosure once they enroll a product. Because Vine reviews are typically the *first* reviews a new listing accumulates, sellers still have an incentive to use Vine tactically to front-load ratings before organic reviews arrive, even though Amazon (not the seller) selects the reviewer and Amazon does not accept seller payment for placement.
- **Mechanism:** Structural disclosure (mandatory badge) + Amazon-side reviewer selection reduces (does not eliminate) the classic "paid positive review" conflict of interest; the front-loading incentive is about *timing* of legitimate signal, not fabrication.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** Any review carrying the "Vine Customer Review of a Free Product" tag is, by definition, an incentivized-but-disclosed review — the agent should read it as "free product, no purchase decision pressure," not weight it identically to an organic paid-purchase review, and should check whether a listing's *early* review pool is disproportionately Vine-tagged versus later reviews.
- **Strength:** Official platform documentation (primary) for the disclosure mechanism; secondary/fact-check-site sourcing for the strategic-use interpretation.
- **Source(s):** Amazon, "Community Guidelines," https://www.amazon.com/gp/help/customer/display.html?nodeId=GLHXEX85MENUE4XF (current, confirms mandatory badge language); Factually.co fact-check, "Are Amazon Vine 'Free Product' Reviews Reliable?", https://factually.co/fact-checks/business/are-amazon-vine-reviews-of-free-products-reliable-a338fd (undated, ~2025/2026).

### F14. ASIN merging / review hijacking moves an entirely unrelated review history onto a new listing
- **Claim:** Sellers (or malicious actors) can merge a new/different product's ASIN into an existing "variation" family, or take over an old ASIN and change its brand/listing details, inheriting hundreds or thousands of reviews that have nothing to do with the actual product being sold. This is reported extensively in Amazon's own seller forums as an ongoing, difficult-to-remediate abuse vector, with sellers reporting 40–50% sales-share losses when a hijacked competitor appears with an inherited review base.
- **Mechanism:** Amazon's variation/merge system is designed for legitimate size/color variants of the *same* product line sharing a review pool; it is exploited by forcibly merging unrelated ASINs to import review volume instantly rather than earning it.

### F22. npm/package-registry download counts can be inflated by anyone with a laptop in about an hour ("download pumping")
- **Claim:** Tenable researchers demonstrated that npm's public download-count metric can be trivially inflated by sending repeated HTTP requests directly to a package's tarball URL — no npm account compromise or special tooling required — inflating a test package to 17,000 downloads in roughly an hour from a standard office laptop. ### F23. US FTC 16 CFR Part 465 (effective Oct 21, 2024) bans fake/bought reviews and undisclosed insider reviews, with per-violation penalties around $51–53K
- **Claim:** The FTC's "Rule on the Use of Consumer Reviews and Testimonials," 16 CFR Part 465, was finalized Aug 14, 2024 (Federal Register publication Aug 22, 2024) and took effect October 21, 2024. - **Mechanism:** This is a *binding rule* (not just voluntary Guides) under FTC Act Section 18, giving the FTC direct civil-penalty authority without having to separately prove the practice was "unfair or deceptive" case-by-case the way the older Endorsement Guides (F24) required.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** Rule Section 465.3 bans fake reviews outright, 465.4 bans buying sentiment-contingent reviews, 465.5 bans undisclosed insider reviews, 465.6 bans review suppression while claiming completeness. An agent evaluating a US-facing site post-Oct-2024 can treat the mere existence of a functioning, unfiltered-looking negative-review section as a weak positive signal (site is not obviously violating 465.6), and treat "this reflects all customer feedback" claims as a specific, legally load-bearing statement worth spot-checking (compare review count against any other public indicator, e.g. a linked forum or social-media complaint volume, for gross mismatch).
- **Strength:** Regulator primary source (Federal Register, eCFR, FTC.gov) — highest possible strength tier for a legal-text claim.
- **Source(s):** Federal Register, "Trade Regulation Rule on the Use of Consumer Reviews and Testimonials," https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/08/22/2024-18519/trade-regulation-rule-on-the-use-of-consumer-reviews-and-testimonials (Aug 22, 2024); eCFR, 16 CFR Part 465, https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-D/part-465 (current, consolidated); FTC, "The Consumer Reviews and Testimonials Rule: Questions and Answers," https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/consumer-reviews-testimonials-rule-questions-answers (May 2025).

### F24. US FTC Endorsement Guides (16 CFR Part 255) require disclosure of any "material connection" not already expected by the audience
- **Claim:** 16 CFR 255.5 requires disclosure of any connection between an endorser and a seller that "might materially affect the weight or credibility of the endorsement" and that a reasonable audience would not already expect (a paid celebrity spokesperson's compensation is the classic *expected* exception that need not be separately disclosed unless the ad implies the endorsement was unpaid). ### F26. EU Omnibus Directive (2019/2161) bans fake reviews outright and forces a disclose-your-verification-method obligation via UCPD Article 7
- **Claim:** Directive (EU) 2019/2161 (adopted Nov 2019, applicable from May 28, 2022) amended the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive (2005/29/EC) to add to Annex I's automatically-unfair "blacklist": submitting or commissioning fake consumer reviews/endorsements (including fake social-media "likes"), and manipulating reviews such as publishing only positive ones while deleting negative ones. Article 31 requires marketplaces to make "reasonable efforts to randomly check" listed products/services against official illegal-product databases.
- **Mechanism:** The DSA targets platform-level structural accountability (who is selling, how content gets ranked) rather than review content directly — it's a complementary layer to the Omnibus Directive/UCPD's content-focused rules (F26).
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** Checkable: does the marketplace listing show verified trader identity/registration details (DSA Art. 30 requires this to be displayed)? Does the platform's terms-of-service/help section describe, even at a high level, how "top" or "most helpful" reviews are selected (DSA Art. 27 requires disclosure of the main ranking parameters)? Absence of either is a compliance gap for EU-facing platforms.
- **Strength:** Regulator/EU-legislative primary source, with mixed secondary sourcing for interpretation (law-firm client alerts).
- **Source(s):** DSA official text portal, Article 30, https://www.eu-digital-services-act.com/Digital_Services_Act_Article_30.html (current, mirrors Regulation (EU) 2022/2065); Freshfields, "DSA decoded #9: The DSA and online marketplaces," https://technologyquotient.freshfields.com/post/102lx12/dsa-decoded-9-the-dsa-and-online-marketplaces (2025); Oxford Academic on Article 27, https://academic.oup.com/ijlit/article/doi/10.1093/ijlit/eaaf004/8133991 (Jan 2025).

### F28. UK DMCC Act 2024 (Schedule 20) puts fake reviews on the automatic-unfair "blacklist" from April 2025; CMA has opened its first formal cases
- **Claim:** The Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 amended Schedule 20 (the UK's equivalent unfair-practices blacklist, post-Brexit successor to the UCPD's Annex I within UK law) via Paragraph 13, adding four banned practices: submitting or commissioning fake reviews; submitting or commissioning reviews that conceal a paid incentive; publishing reviews in a misleading way or without appropriate verification procedures; and offering/providing services that facilitate any of the above (i.e., review-farm brokers are directly, independently liable, not just their clients). The Act defines a "fake consumer review" as one purporting to be, but not actually being, based on a person's genuine experience, and defines a review as "concealing" incentivization if the fact of commissioning is not made apparent. These provisions came into force April 6, 2025; the CMA published detailed compliance guidance (CMA208) on April 4, 2025, recommending businesses maintain (a) a clear policy on preventing/removing false review content and (b) a documented risk assessment with proportionate mitigation steps. Because it's a blacklist entry, the CMA does not need to separately prove consumer harm/decision-influence to enforce — same structural advantage as the EU version. Penalties can reach 10% of global turnover. On March 27, 2026 the CMA announced its first formal investigations under these specific provisions.
- **Mechanism:** Blacklist-based strict liability plus explicit platform ("publisher") obligations — publishers (marketplaces, specialist review sites, search services that display third-party reviews) must themselves maintain risk-proportionate prevention/removal processes, not just avoid directly committing the practices.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** For UK-facing sites: does the platform publish a stated fake-review prevention/removal policy (CMA's guidance specifically recommends this be documented and findable)? A missing or absent stated policy is a compliance gap under the CMA's own recommended baseline, though CMA208 is guidance, not itself a strict legal requirement to publish the policy publicly.
- **Strength:** Regulator/UK-legislative primary source (legislation.gov.uk, gov.uk CMA guidance PDF).
- **Source(s):** Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024, Schedule 20, https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2024/13/schedule/20 (current, consolidated); CMA, "Fake reviews: CMA208" guidance, https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/67eeb64fe9c76fa33048c790/CMA208_-_Fake_reviews_guidance.pdf (Apr 4, 2025); CMA enforcement update via law-firm alert, https://www.tlt.com/insights-and-events/insight/cma-steps-up-its-crackdown-on-fake-and-misleading-reviews (referencing Mar 27, 2026 announcement — locate CMA's own press release for final citation).

### F29. France: AFNOR NF Z74-501 → ISO 20488 is a voluntary certification standard for review-collection processes; DGCCRF enforces the underlying criminal fraud law
- **Claim:** AFNOR published the world's first standard governing online consumer reviews, NF Z74-501, in July 2013, covering collection/moderation/restitution processes (e.g., banning outright purchase of reviews, requiring recency-based ordering, requiring transparency about calculation methods, requiring full-text display of reviews) across the whole digital economy, not just hospitality. It was superseded in September 2018 by an international version, ISO 20488 (adopted by AFNOR as NF ISO 20488). Adoption/certification ("NF Service – Avis en Ligne") is entirely voluntary — it is a certification mark, not a legal requirement — but DGCCRF (France's consumer/competition fraud regulator) has specifically flagged cases of businesses falsely claiming NF Z74-501 compliance without actually being certified. Separately and independently of the standard, DGCCRF investigations into fake-review-writing services found the underlying conduct (deceptive commercial practices, e.g., paying for/fabricating reviews) constitutes a criminal offense under French consumer law, punishable by up to 2 years' imprisonment and a €300,000 fine.
- **Mechanism:** The AFNOR/ISO standard is a *voluntary quality-assurance certification* (comparable to an ISO-9001-style process audit) layered on top of, not a substitute for, the binding French/EU deceptive-practices criminal law that DGCCRF actually enforces.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** Look for an explicit "NF Service – Avis en Ligne" or "ISO 20488" certification mark/claim on a French-facing review section — if present, check whether it links to an actual AFNOR certification record (DGCCRF has specifically prosecuted false claims of this certification); absence of the mark is not itself meaningful (certification is optional and most sites don't have it), but a *false* claim of certification is a documented, prosecutable fraud pattern in France specifically.
- **Strength:** Standards-body primary source (AFNOR boutique listing) for the standard's existence/scope; regulator primary source (economie.gouv.fr, the ministry housing DGCCRF) for the enforcement/criminal-penalty claim.
- **Source(s):** AFNOR, NF Z74-501 standard listing, https://www.boutique.afnor.org/en-gb/standard/nf-z74501/online-consumer-reviews-principles-and-requirements-for-collection-moderati/fa178349/1365 (current); DGCCRF, "Avis en ligne : attention aux faux commentaires !", https://www.economie.gouv.fr/dgccrf/les-fiches-pratiques/avis-en-ligne-attention-aux-faux-commentaires (Mar 27, 2024) — this is the strongest primary source in the French-language cluster and directly states the criminal-penalty figures; Nomos legal-analysis summary of the false-certification-claim enforcement pattern, https://www.nomosparis.com/enquete-de-la-dgccrf-sur-les-faux-avis-de-consommateurs/ (secondary, dated ~2018, re-verify currency).

### F30. Fakespot is fully dead as of July 1, 2025 — do not direct an agent to use it

### F31. ReviewMeta and TheReviewIndex are also dead/unreliable as of 2025–2026 — do not direct an agent to either
- **Claim:** ReviewMeta (an independent Amazon-review statistical-anomaly analyzer, historically checking review-date clustering, unverified-purchase share, and word-count consistency) went offline in early 2026 with no official shutdown statement from its operators; legacy browser extensions may still load but return errors or stale data and are explicitly described by multiple sources as unreliable for current use. TheReviewIndex (a separate neural-network-based Amazon review analyzer producing an "Overview score" and "Spam Test" result) also shut down in the 2025–2026 window, with the operator attributing the closure to Amazon policy changes restricting the data access these third-party tools relied on.
- **Mechanism:** Multiple secondary sources converge on the same underlying cause independent of any one operator's stated reason: Amazon (and similarly other platforms) progressively restricted the scraping/API access that all of these third-party analyzers depended on to pull review data at scale, and separately, review-fraud tactics (AI-generated text, brushing) evolved past what these tools' older statistical methodologies (built for the 2017–2022 fraud landscape) could usefully flag.
- **Strength:** No primary shutdown announcement was located for either tool in this research pass (unlike Fakespot's Mozilla blog post) — this finding rests on convergent secondary/tertiary reporting (tech blogs, "alternative to X" comparison sites) describing sites that no longer load. Treat the *fact of non-functionality* as high-confidence (multiple independent, recent, non-affiliated sources all describe the same dead-site symptom) but the *stated reasons* as lower-confidence interpretation.
- **Source(s):** RateBud blog, "Fakespot Is Shutting Down: The Best Free Alternatives for 2026," https://www.ratebud.ai/blog/fakespot-shutting-down-best-alternatives-2026 (Feb 2026, states ReviewMeta appears offline); FakeFind blog, "ReviewMeta Is Offline — The Best Free Alternative in 2026," https://fakefind.ai/blog/reviewmeta-alternative/ (2026); SellerSonar, "Amazon Review Checker To Find Fake Reviews," https://sellersonar.com/blog/amazon-review-checker/ (May 2026, states TheReviewIndex "announced it is permanently down due to Amazon policy changes"); Yahoo Tech, "Fake Amazon Reviews Are Everywhere...", https://tech.yahoo.com/general/articles/fake-amazon-reviews-everywhere-tips-110000039.html (Mar 2026, independently confirms all three tools "disappeared in the last year").
- **Caveats / false positives:** Because none of these citing sources are the tools' own operators (unlike Fakespot/Mozilla), and several are themselves marketing content for *replacement* paid tools (RateBud, FakeFind) with an obvious commercial incentive to declare the old tools dead, the skill should note this status as "very likely dead, multiple independent confirmations, but no primary source located" rather than citing it with the same confidence as F30.

### F32. Review bombing is a distinct phenomenon from fraud: coordinated, often genuine, off-topic negative reviews driven by ideology/controversy rather than product experience
- **Claim:** Academic literature defines "review bombing" as coordinated posting of a disproportionate volume of negative reviews to depress an aggregate score, typically triggered by an external controversy (a political statement, a casting/creative decision, a corporate action) rather than the product itself, and typically identifiable by sudden temporal clustering plus content that references the controversy rather than the product experience. An NLP study of 50,000+ Metacritic PC game user-score aggregations built a classifier distinguishing "review bombing" from ordinary bad ratings, using criteria including a ≥4.0-point gap between critic Metascore and average user score as a candidate-flagging threshold. A dedicated case study of "The Last of Us Part II" quantified this dynamic in detail.
- **Mechanism:** Unlike fraud-for-profit fake reviews, review-bombing participants are typically real users expressing genuine (if off-topic or disproportionate) sentiment — the manipulation is in the *coordination and framing*, not in reviewer authenticity.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** A sharp negative-rating spike correlated with a datable public controversy (checkable via a quick news search for the product/company name + date of the spike), combined with review text referencing the controversy rather than product attributes, is the distinguishing signature versus F6's fraud-burst pattern (which correlates with commissioned campaigns, not public news events) or F4's competitor-sabotage pattern (which doesn't reference a public controversy at all).
- **Strength:** Peer-reviewed (Quality & Quantity journal; arXiv NLP study, not yet in a numbered peer-reviewed venue at time of citation — verify current publication status).
- **Source(s):** Cardellino-adjacent, "Review bombing: ideology-driven polarisation in online ratings: The case study of The Last of Us (part II)," Quality & Quantity, https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11135-024-01981-z (Sep 2024); "A NLP Approach to 'Review Bombing' in Metacritic PC Videogames User Ratings," arXiv:2405.06306, https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.06306 (May 2024).
- **Caveats / false positives:** This finding matters primarily as a **negative control** for the skill: do not classify a genuine, controversy-driven ratings collapse as "fake reviews" — it calls for different downstream advice (discount the *magnitude* of the swing but not necessarily every individual review's authenticity) than fabricated-review fraud does.

## Operational checklist candidates

1. Before judging a star average, check the full star-by-star histogram, not just the mean; expect a J-shaped baseline (F10) and treat a *missing* 1–3 star tail (near-pure 5-star with no complaint spike at all) as more anomalous than a merely-high average.
2. Weight review *count* and *distribution shape* above the raw mean when comparing two listings; per F11, the mean alone correlates weakly with independently-measured quality, and is inflated by brand/price effects independent of quality.
3. Treat "Verified Purchase" / "verified guest" badges as raising confidence, not proving authenticity — check for the specific gaming vectors documented for that platform (Amazon brushing, F12; Booking.com cash-marked non-stays, F19) before treating verification as decisive.
4. Look for a visible date/time axis on reviews and check for burst clustering — a spike in review volume in a narrow window, especially one skewed toward one star value, is a stronger fraud signal than any individual review's content (F6); but first check whether the spike aligns with a dated public controversy (review-bombing pattern, F32) or an organic event (press feature, sale) before concluding fraud.
5. Where the platform exposes individual reviewer profiles (Amazon, Yelp, Google Local Guides), sample a handful of reviewers on a suspicious listing and check whether they show singleton/near-singleton, recently-created, or narrowly-clustered review histories (F1, F3, F5).
6. Check for near-duplicate phrasing across multiple reviews on the same listing, and across different reviewers if profiles are browsable (F1, F5) — structural/paragraph-level similarity, not just word choice, given LLM paraphrasing (F8).
7. Actively look for the platform's own first-party fraud-detection artifacts before doing anything else: Yelp Consumer Alert banners and the "not currently recommended" review count (F17); Google's "reviews paused" banner (F16); Trustpilot's "verified" tag on individual reviews (F15) — these are the platform's own conclusion, already computed, and cheaper to check than reconstructing the signal yourself.
8. For US-facing sites post-Oct-2024, check whether the site's review section plausibly complies with 16 CFR 465 — does it claim to show "all" reviews while an obviously-filtered/all-positive pattern suggests otherwise (F23, cf. Fashion Nova precedent F25)?
9. For EU-facing sites, look for an explicit statement of how reviews are verified (required by amended UCPD Article 7, F26) and, for marketplaces, visible trader-identity information on the listing (DSA Article 30, F27); treat their complete absence as a documented compliance gap worth flagging, not proof of fraud.
10. For UK-facing sites post-April-2025, check for a stated fake-review prevention policy, which CMA guidance (F28) recommends businesses publish.
11. For B2B software review sites, check whether reviews carry an incentive-disclosure tag (F20) and read incentivized reviews as response-bias-skewed (toward casual users willing to spend a few minutes for a small reward) rather than as sentiment-biased or fake outright.
12. For GitHub/npm/PyPI, cross-check the headline popularity metric (stars, downloads) against independent adoption evidence — real issues/PRs/forks, other projects listing it as a dependency, and a sane version-release cadence (F21, F22) — rather than trusting the headline number alone.
13. Never instruct an agent to visit Fakespot, ReviewMeta, or TheReviewIndex — all are dead or non-functional as of 2025–2026 (F30, F31).
14. When a rating collapse coincides with a dated public controversy and review text references that controversy rather than the product, classify it as likely review-bombing (discount magnitude, but don't assume every individual review is fabricated) rather than commissioned fraud (F32).

## Dead ends and myths

1. **"A 5.0 (or near-5.0) average is inherently suspicious / real products always have some complaints."** Not supported as a standalone rule. Hu, Pavlou & Zhang (F10) show rating distributions are structurally J-shaped (skewed toward 5-star, with a smaller 1-star spike, minimal middle) *even with zero fraud*, purely from acquisition and under-reporting self-selection. A perfect-looking average is common and often organic, especially for low-review-count or niche/high-fit products. What's actually anomalous is a *missing complaint tail* (near-zero 1–4 star with no spike at all, not just a high mean) — the skill must teach that more precise pattern, not "suspiciously high average" as a blanket heuristic. Source: https://aisel.aisnet.org/misq/vol41/iss2/8/ (2017).
2. **"More reviews automatically means more trustworthy / harder to fake."** Not supported unconditionally. He, Hollenbeck & Proserpio (F7) specifically found that products with *already many reviews and high ratings* still purchase fake reviews — volume is not itself exculpatory, and fraud campaigns explicitly target already-successful listings, not only cold-start ones. Source: https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mksc.2022.1353 (2022, Marketing Science).
3. **"A 'Verified Purchase' / verified-guest badge means the review is genuine."** Not supported. Amazon's brushing scheme (F12) produces a bona fide "Verified Purchase" label on a review written by someone who never wanted, chose, or evaluated the product on its merits — verification checks that a transaction occurred, not that the reviewer had an authentic evaluative experience. Booking.com's booking-gated review system (F19) has an analogous gap via cash-marked, never-fulfilled reservations. Sources: https://www.bbb.org/article/news-releases/20509-amazon-brushing-scam-indicates-a-serious-problem-for-victims (2024); https://www.booking.com/content-moderation-policy/guest-reviews-standards.html.
4. **"Detailed, long, specific-sounding reviews are more trustworthy than short generic ones."** This heuristic was defensible pre-2023 (Ott et al., F2, found genuine reviews skew toward concrete/informative detail vs. deceptive reviews' narrative/imaginative style) but is now unreliable as a standalone rule: 2024–2026 research on LLM-generated fake reviews (F8) shows AI-written fake reviews can be prompted to include exactly the concrete, specific detail that used to distinguish genuine reviews, and multiple 2025 studies found LLM-written fake reviews near-indistinguishable from genuine ones in blind human/machine tests. Source: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.13313 (2025).
5. **"Third-party browser extensions like Fakespot/ReviewMeta will tell you if reviews are fake."** These tools are dead as of 2025–2026 (F30, F31) — Fakespot shut down entirely by Mozilla on July 1, 2025 (confirmed via Mozilla's own blog), and ReviewMeta/TheReviewIndex are independently reported offline/non-functional in 2025–2026 coverage. Any advice pointing an agent at these specific tools will fail. Source: https://blog.mozilla.org/en/mozilla/building-whats-next/ (May 2025).
6. **"Reading only the 1-star reviews reveals the 'real truth' about a product."** Not well-supported as a universal rule. Negative reviews are subject to their own selection and manipulation dynamics symmetric to positive ones: under-reporting bias also applies to 1-star reviews (they're written by people who "moan," not necessarily a representative sample of dissatisfied buyers, per F10); competitors can commission fake-negative reviews to sabotage a rival (F4, Luca & Zervas found competition increases the odds of receiving unfavorable *fake* reviews specifically); and coordinated review-bombing (F32) produces large volumes of genuine-but-off-topic 1-star reviews driven by controversy rather than product defects. Low ratings deserve scrutiny for the *same* fraud/bias patterns as high ratings, not blanket trust. Sources: https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2015.2304 (2016); https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11135-024-01981-z (2024).
7. **"No reviews / very few reviews always means a new, unproven, or bad product."** Not supported as a blanket rule — F4 shows low review count correlates with *higher fraud incentive* (weak reputation → more likely to buy fake reviews), but a niche/prosumer/recently-launched product can be entirely legitimate with few organic reviews; the correct inference is "raise scrutiny of what reviews do exist," not "assume defect." No single dedicated source states this as a formal finding; flagged here as an inference from F4 plus general self-selection literature (F10) rather than a directly cited myth-debunking study — treat with appropriate caution in the skill text.

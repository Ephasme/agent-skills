# Slice D — Primary, hard-to-fake data sources about real quality, reliability and longevity — research findings

## Executive summary

1. Reviews are the *weakest* tier of evidence because they measure sentiment at time-of-writing, not survival over years, and are the easiest artifact to fake at scale. The agent's default move should be: identify the category of the item (hardware/vehicle/appliance/software/service/trade), then reach for the matching primary source below *before* reading any star rating.
2. For durable hardware, the strongest agent-observable proxies are **failure/return data from people who see thousands of units** (Backblaze for drives, Puget Systems for PC components, TÜV/ADAC for cars) — not because they're perfect (they all have selection bias) but because the bias is *documented and constant*, unlike review fraud which is adversarial and adaptive.
3. Government and standards databases (EPREL, EU Safety Gate, Rappel Conso, CPSC, NHTSA, FCC ID, UL Product iQ) are close to unfakeable: a private party cannot forge an entry, and absence-of-listing or absence-of-recall is itself informative only when combined with market-size context (a product with 3 units sold will never generate a recall regardless of quality).
4. Star ratings and "5.0/5" scores are not evidence of quality; they are evidence of *low review count* or *manipulated review count*. A handful of exact-source data points already collected by RatingStatistics/ReviewFraudSignals slices should be cross-checked against this slice's primary sources rather than trusted alone.
5. JD Power IQS/VDS, Consumer Reports, Which?, Stiftung Warentest, ADAC, TÜV are all **survey aggregators**, not lab measurements — treat their outputs as "strength: industry report" not "peer-reviewed," and always check N and weighting method before citing a number.
6. For software/services, the two most under-used objective signals are (a) the **security-update support-window commitment** (does the vendor even say when it stops patching?) and (b) **status-page incident history**, both of which are directly observable without asking the vendor anything.
7. GitHub stars and npm/PyPI download counts are both *actively and cheaply gamed* in 2024–2026 (StarScout found ~6M suspected fake GitHub stars; npm download-count spoofing is trivial and documented). Neither should be used as a standalone signal; use OpenSSF Scorecard + deps.dev + endoflife.date + issue-response latency instead.
8. "Abandoned but still popular" open-source projects are detectable only via a **multi-signal check** (last commit, release cadence, issue response latency, maintainer activity elsewhere, dependency freshness) — no single metric is reliable, including commit recency alone.
9. Repairability and durability regulation is bifurcating fast: the EU now runs *two* French repairability regimes in parallel during 2025 (`indice de réparabilité` being phased out category by category in favor of `indice de durabilité`), an EU-wide Right to Repair Directive that started applying 31 July 2026, and the EU Cyber Resilience Act imposing a minimum 5-year security-support floor on connected products from ~Sept 2026 onward — the agent must check *product category and date* before assuming which regime applies.
10. Secondhand resale price after N years is a legitimate crowd-sourced durability proxy (recognized in finance/appraisal literature) but is confounded by brand desirability and fashion, not just durability — use it as a corroborating signal, never standalone.
11. For contractors/tradespeople, licence-board lookups and company-registry/insolvency filings are the closest thing to "hard to fake" evidence available to a consumer-facing agent — court/insolvency records lag reality by days to weeks, so a clean record is weaker evidence than a bad one (absence of evidence ≠ evidence of absence, but presence of a red flag is a strong flag).
12. Warranty length is a genuine economic signal (grounded in Spence-style signaling theory) but its strength depends on brand reputation being credible in the first place — a long warranty from a fly-by-night brand is not informative, per the empirical marketing literature cited below.
13. Total-cost-of-ownership analysis (e.g., printer ink, contractor consumables, SaaS overage pricing) frequently dwarfs sticker price and is independently verifiable by the agent via published consumable pricing — this is one of the few "quality" questions an agent can fully resolve through arithmetic rather than trust.
14. Every regulatory database has scope limits the agent must state: e.g., FCC ID lookup only works for RF-emitting devices explicitly certified (not covered under Supplier's Declaration of Conformity); NHTSA/EU recall databases only catch defects severe enough to trigger regulatory action; UL/CE logos can be counterfeited on the package even when the underlying certification database entry is real or absent — always cross-check the logo against the database, never trust the logo alone.
15. Class-action dockets and insolvency filings are asymmetric evidence: many lawsuits are frivolous and many companies are sued regardless of quality, so the informative signal is *pattern* (repeated, similar, successful claims against the same defect) not "has ever been sued."

## Findings

### F1. Backblaze Drive Stats — hard drive/SSD failure rates by model
- **Claim:** Backblaze publishes quarterly and annual failure-rate (AFR) statistics for every hard-drive and SSD model in its data centers, broken out by manufacturer and model, based on tens of thousands of drives in continuous production use.
- **Mechanism:** Backblaze operates a very large storage fleet and logs every drive failure (SMART stats + replacement events) as an operational byproduct of running the business, making the incentive to over- or under-report roughly zero (it's their own uptime at stake).
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** Compute AFR = (drive failures during period) / (average number of drives in service during period), annualized. Backblaze publishes this pre-computed per model with drive-count and drive-days, so an agent can sanity-check "is the model's sample size (drive-days) large enough to trust the rate" (models with <a few thousand drive-days should be flagged low-confidence).
- **Strength:** Industry report (single large operator, not peer-reviewed, but methodology and raw data are public).
- **Access:** Free (public web dashboard + downloadable raw CSV datasets, no login).
- **Source(s):** Backblaze Drive Stats hub, https://www.backblaze.com/cloud-storage/resources/hard-drive-test-data (accessed 2026-08-19); 2025 Drive Stats report, https://www.backblaze.com/blog/backblaze-drive-stats-for-2025/ (Feb 12, 2026).
- **Caveats / false positives:** Backblaze's fleet is enterprise/datacenter-grade drives run in RAID-like pods with specific cooling/vibration/duty-cycle profiles — results do not directly transfer to a single consumer drive in a desktop PC or NAS. Small-sample models (new models just added) show noisy AFR. Only covers drives Backblaze actually buys, i.e. skews to models available at enterprise volume.

### F2. Puget Systems Hardware Reliability Report — PC component failure/RMA rates
- **Claim:** Puget Systems (a boutique PC system builder) publishes annual failure rates by GPU/CPU/RAM/SSD model and brand, based on burn-in testing plus in-field RMA data across the workstations it builds and sells.
- **Mechanism:** Every unit is burned in before shipping and every field failure is tracked via Puget's own RMA process, giving a consistent measurement funnel across brands within a given year.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** Compare within-report relative rankings (e.g., Brand A GPU failure rate vs Brand B in the same generation), not absolute percentages across years, since methodology has been revised (e.g., 2022 methodology change).
- **Strength:** Industry report (single reseller, explicitly non-representative per their own disclaimer).
- **Access:** Free (public blog article; no login).
- **Source(s):** Puget Systems, "Most Reliable Hardware of 2025," https://www.pugetsystems.com/labs/articles/puget-systems-most-reliable-hardware-of-2025/ (Feb 2, 2026); coverage: https://www.techspot.com/news/111160-most-reliable-pc-hardware-2025-according-puget-systems.html.
- **Caveats / false positives:** Puget explicitly states this "is based on our experience... and does not cover the entire computer hardware industry"; their build standards (better cooling, careful assembly, pre-ship burn-in) filter out infant-mortality failures that a DIY builder or a cheap prebuilt would encounter, so absolute failure rates likely understate real-world consumer rates even though relative brand rankings are informative.

### F3. Mindfactory RMA/return-rate publication — DEAD END for current data
- **Claim:** German retailer Mindfactory GmbH's return/RMA rates by GPU model were historically informally tracked and occasionally leaked/scraped, feeding widely-cited PC-hardware "reliability" charts online. 3DCenter.org's quarterly Mindfactory reports cover only *unit sales volume and market share*, not return/defect rates.
- **Mechanism:** N/A — this is a documentation of what does NOT currently exist, not a mechanism.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** None currently reliable; do not cite "Mindfactory Ausfallquote" charts found via image search without a live, dated primary source — most circulating in enthusiast forums as of 2024 are old (pre-2020) leaked spreadsheets being recirculated as if current.
- **Strength:** N/A (negative finding).
- **Access:** Free (public web article) — but see caveat: no current RMA-rate data exists to access.
- **Source(s):** 3DCenter.org quarterly reports (sales-only), e.g. https://www.3dcenter.org/artikel/grafikkarten-verkaufsstatistik-mindfactory-q22024 (Sept 8, 2024) — confirms these reports track only Stückzahlen (unit counts), not defect/RMA rates.
- **Caveats / false positives:** `UNVERIFIED-STATUS` — could not confirm any currently-live, dated Mindfactory RMA/Ausfallquote publication as of 2026-08-19. Treat any "Mindfactory reliability chart" the agent encounters as suspect until it can trace it to a specific dated primary release.

### F4. Stiftung Warentest reader surveys — appliance reliability and repair-success rates
- **Claim:** Germany's Stiftung Warentest runs large-N reader surveys (~14,450 respondents on washing machines/dryers/dishwashers; >10,000 respondents / >90,000 devices on a separate repair survey) measuring device failure timing, defect type, and whether repair attempts succeeded.
- **Mechanism:** Self-reported panel survey of Test.de readers/subscribers, published with explicit methodology page describing question wording and sample composition.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** The survey reports median time-to-first-failure by product category (e.g., mobile phones ~1 year, washing machines ~5 years, vacuum cleaners ~8 years) and repair-success rate (more than half of repair attempts in the survey failed, despite being attempted for ~3/4 of defects) — usable as category-level base rates to sanity-check an individual product's claimed lifespan.
- **Strength:** Industry report / large-panel survey, methodology disclosed but self-selected respondent pool (Test.de readers skew toward more engaged, possibly more critical, consumers).
- **Access:** Paywalled for full test.de articles/back-issues; survey methodology and headline results summarized free.
- **Source(s):** "Umfrage Haushaltsgeräte: So sind wir vorgegangen," https://www.test.de/Umfrage-Haushaltsgeraete-Das-sind-die-zuverlaessigsten-Marken-5298604-5298608/ (Aug 17, 2021); "Ergebnisse Reparatur-Umfrage," https://www.test.de/Ergebnisse-Reparatur-Umfrage-Erfahrungen-von-10000-Teilnehmern-ausgewertet-5587855-0/ (Nov 4, 2021).
- **Caveats / false positives:** Self-selected panel, not a probability sample; German market only (brand mix and repair-shop availability differ from other markets); dates are 2021, may be stale for volatile categories like smartphones — check for a newer wave before citing.

### F5. Which? UK car reliability survey — large-N owner survey with severity weighting
- **Claim:** Which? surveys tens of thousands of UK car owners annually (most recent cited: 63,334 drivers on 72,470 cars) and computes reliability scores weighting fault *seriousness*, not just fault count, broken out by vehicle age band (0–4y, 5–9y, 10–15y).
- **Mechanism:** Owner self-report panel, standardized fault-severity weighting scheme (methodology summarized on their results page, full methodology not fully public).
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** Compare reliability score band-by-age (a car scoring well at 0–4y but poorly at 10–15y flags a longevity problem invisible to a "years owned <2" review-site sample).
- **Strength:** Industry report (subscriber survey, weighting methodology partially disclosed).
- **Access:** Paywalled — full car reliability survey results require a Which? subscription; some headline findings summarized free.
- **Source(s):** "Most reliable cars to buy in the UK for 2026," https://www.which.co.uk/reviews/new-and-used-cars/article/car-reliability/most-reliable-cars-adRx52m7zuhV (June 9, 2026).
- **Caveats / false positives:** UK market and driving conditions only; self-selected Which? subscriber base skews toward more affluent, safety-conscious owners; exact severity-weighting formula not published, so scores can't be independently recomputed — treat as directional, not a hard number.

### F6. TÜV Report (Germany) — mandatory roadworthiness inspection defect rates
- **Claim:** TÜV-Verband aggregates results of ~9.5 million mandatory *Hauptuntersuchung* (HU) roadworthiness inspections in Germany, publishing defect rates ("Mängelquote") by model and by vehicle age, including a percentage failing with "erhebliche" (significant) or "gefährliche" (dangerous) defects.
- **Mechanism:** Every registered car in Germany undergoes HU inspection every 1–2 years by law; TÜV/DEKRA/GTÜ inspectors record standardized defect categories, and TÜV-Verband aggregates its own inspectors' results into the published report — this is measured physical inspection data, not self-report.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** Age-adjusted defect rate at the 2–3-year mark is the headline "quality at delivery" metric; defect rate at 10-15 years is the longevity metric; the report explicitly shows defect rate roughly tripling from 10% (5y) to 32.9% (15y) — an agent can benchmark a specific model's stated defect rate against these population baselines.
- **Strength:** Regulator-adjacent (published by the TÜV industry association based on mandatory statutory inspections, not an independent regulator, but the underlying inspection is a legal requirement carried out by an accredited body).
- **Access:** Free press release/summary; the full TÜV-Report PDF is sold commercially (paywalled) via TÜV SÜD publications.
- **Source(s):** TÜV-Verband press release, "TÜV-Report 2026: Mängelquoten steigen zum vierten Mal in Folge," https://www.tuev-verband.de/pressemitteilungen/tuev-report-2026 (Nov 20, 2025); publication hub https://www.tuvsud.com/de-de/publikationen/tuev-report.
- **Caveats / false positives:** German-market vehicle mix and usage patterns only; only covers *inspectable, common defect categories* (won't catch e.g. infotainment software bugs that don't affect roadworthiness); TÜV is itself a private-sector body (albeit statutorily accredited) with a documented 2014 credibility controversy (see Statista citation in search results) around unrelated club governance — not a defect-data integrity issue but worth knowing before treating TÜV as a pure "government" source.

### F7. ADAC Pannenstatistik (Germany) — roadside-breakdown-call failure rate by model
- **Claim:** ADAC (Germany's largest automobile club) publishes an annual breakdown-call statistic ("Pannenkennziffer" = breakdowns per 1,000 registered vehicles) by model and model-year, based on >3.5 million roadside-assistance callouts per year, restricted to models with ≥5,000 registered units to avoid small-sample noise.
- **Mechanism:** Every ADAC roadside callout is logged with cause-of-breakdown taxonomy (battery, engine/engine-management, alternator/starter/electrics, etc.), aggregated per vehicle model and normalized by fleet size.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** Pannenkennziffer = breakdowns / registered vehicles (per 1,000), computed per age-cohort; ADAC applies a percentile-based color code (bottom 15% dark green ... worst 5% red) so an agent can read off relative standing without needing raw numbers.
- **Strength:** Industry report (auto club, not government, but very large N and disclosed minimum-fleet-size threshold to reduce noise).
- **Access:** Free (public press releases and methodology page).
- **Source(s):** ADAC methodology page, https://www.adac.de/infotestrat/unfall-schaeden-und-panne/pannenstatistik/pannenstatistik_methodik_hintergrund.aspx (2025); "Gelbe Engel: Alle 9 Sekunden ein Einsatz" (fleet totals), https://presse.adac.de/meldungen/adac-ev/clubleistungen/gelbe-engel-alle-9-sekunden-ein-einsatz.html.
- **Caveats / false positives:** Battery-related breakdowns (44.1% of all callouts) dominate the metric and are heavily influenced by climate/short-trip usage patterns rather than intrinsic vehicle quality — an agent comparing two models should check whether the gap is driven by battery-category failures before concluding "less reliable." German-market only; ADAC membership base may not represent the full vehicle population equally.

### F8. JD Power VDS / IQS — US new-car quality/dependability studies and their documented flaws
- **Claim:** JD Power's Initial Quality Study (IQS, ~78,514 respondents at 90 days ownership) and Vehicle Dependability Study (VDS, ~30,000+ original owners at 3 years) are the most widely cited US new-car quality metrics, using "Problems Per 100 vehicles" (PP100).
- **Mechanism:** Owner self-report survey plus (since recent methodology revisions) integrated real-world dealer repair-visit data.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** PP100, lower = better; but the agent MUST discount this metric for known flaws (see caveats) rather than treat it as a clean severity-adjusted number.
- **Strength:** Industry report (large N, but explicitly criticized methodology).
- **Access:** Free press releases with headline results; full underlying dataset is commercially licensed (paywalled) by JD Power.
- **Source(s):** JD Power 2026 IQS press release, https://www.jdpower.com/business/press-releases/2026-us-initial-quality-study-pr (2026); critical analysis, TrueDelta, "J.D. Power IQS 2017: Who Did Best, And What Does It Mean?", https://www.truedelta.com/blog/?p=2054.
- **Caveats / false positives, documented:** (1) PP100 counts a rattling trim panel and a blown engine as equally "1 problem" — no severity weighting. (2) It conflates *repairable mechanical defects* with *subjective ease-of-use complaints* (e.g., "infotainment confusing" counts same as "engine failed"). (3) It aggregates model-level data up to brand-level rankings, which can misrepresent a brand if one bad model skews the average. (4) It collects **no data on reliability after the factory warranty expires** — a critical blind spot for long-term ownership decisions. (5) Participation is incomplete — e.g. Tesla has been excluded in some study years due to data-access restrictions in certain US states.

### F9. NHTSA — vehicle complaints, Technical Service Bulletins, investigations, and recalls (US)
- **Claim:** NHTSA operates free, public, VIN- and model-searchable databases of consumer complaints, manufacturer Technical Service Bulletins (TSBs), open investigations, and safety recalls for vehicles sold in the US.
- **Mechanism:** Complaints are consumer-submitted (self-report, unverified by NHTSA at submission but subject to investigation); TSBs are manufacturer-issued service guidance NHTSA is required to collect; recalls follow a formal regulatory determination process (either manufacturer-initiated or NHTSA-compelled).
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** Search NHTSA.gov/recalls by VIN for a specific vehicle's outstanding recalls; search "Search Safety Issues" by make/model/year for complaint volume and TSB list; a cluster of complaints describing the *same failure mode* pre-dating a formal recall is a leading indicator worth flagging even before NHTSA acts.
- **Strength:** Regulator (US federal, NHTSA — part of DOT).
- **Access:** Free (US federal government database, no login required).
- **Source(s):** NHTSA VIN recall lookup, https://www.nhtsa.gov/recalls; Search Safety Issues (complaints/TSBs/investigations), https://www.nhtsa.gov/search-safety-issues.
- **Caveats / false positives:** Complaint volume is influenced by fleet size (a popular model will have more raw complaints even at an equal or lower per-vehicle rate) — always normalize by estimated units sold/registered before comparing models. TSBs are not defect admissions and are not publicly indexed as prominently as recalls; only unresolved (open) recalls show on third-party vehicle-history reports like Carfax, so TSBs must be checked directly on NHTSA's own site.

### F10. Recall/dangerous-product registries: Rappel Conso (FR), EU Safety Gate, CPSC SaferProducts.gov (US), EU RASFF (food)
- **Claim:** Four government-run registries make regulatory-grade recall/danger notifications public and (mostly) machine-readable: France's RappelConso, the EU's Safety Gate (formerly RAPEX), the US CPSC's SaferProducts.gov, and the EU's RASFF for food.
- **Mechanism:** Legally mandated notification — manufacturers/importers/distributors in the EU/France are obligated to report; in the US, CPSC both receives consumer harm reports and issues formal recalls; RASFF is a rapid-alert network between EU/EEA food-safety authorities.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** For any specific branded product, search by product name/GTIN/brand in each relevant registry before purchase; a listing = confirmed regulatory action, not an allegation. RappelConso exposes an open, key-free, daily-updated API keyed by GTIN (rare for a French government dataset) making it directly agent-queryable.
- **Strength:** Regulator (France: DGCCRF/DGAL/DGEC/DGPR; EU: European Commission; US: CPSC, an independent federal agency).
- **Access:** Free (government databases; RappelConso additionally offers a free, key-free public API).
- **Source(s):** RappelConso, https://rappel.conso.gouv.fr/ and open dataset https://www.data.gouv.fr/datasets/rappelconso-v2-rappels-de-produits; EU Safety Gate, described at https://www.baua.de/EN/Topics/Monitoring-evaluation/Market-surveillance-and-product-safety/Market-surveillance/Safety-Gate (public search database run by the European Commission); CPSC SaferProducts.gov search, https://www.saferproducts.gov/PublicSearch and API info https://www.cpsc.gov/Recalls/CPSC-Recalls-Application-Program-Interface-API-Information.
- **Caveats / false positives:** Absence from these registries only means "no regulatory action has been triggered yet" — it does NOT mean "safe" or "no defects," especially for low-volume, niche, or recently-launched products. CPSC SaferProducts.gov includes unverified consumer-submitted harm *reports*, not just confirmed recalls — read the record type carefully (report vs. recall).

### F11. iFixit teardown/failure-mode documentation — real disassembly evidence, not marketing claims
- **Claim:** iFixit publishes free teardown guides for a huge range of consumer electronics, documenting internal component choices, common failure points, and battery/board design decisions directly from physical disassembly — independent of any manufacturer claim.
- **Mechanism:** iFixit engineers physically open and photograph devices, cross-referencing internals against known failure patterns from their repair-guide community.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** Search "[product name] teardown iFixit" for evidence of actual internals (e.g., battery glued vs. clip-in, use of proprietary screws, thermal-paste quality) that predicts serviceability and failure mode independent of any spec sheet.
- **Strength:** Vendor-independent methodology, industry-report tier (iFixit is a commercial repair-parts retailer, so has some incentive to emphasize repairability, but teardown photography is primary physical evidence, not opinion).
- **Access:** Free (public teardown guides); iFixit also sells parts/tools commercially, but the documentation itself is free.
- **Source(s):** iFixit teardown/guide library, https://www.ifixit.com (per-product URLs vary; search-driven).
- **Caveats / false positives:** Teardown-based judgments about "flimsy design" are somewhat subjective absent a formal rubric — for a *scored* repairability judgment use F32 (iFixit Repairability Score) instead of raw teardown commentary.

### F12. Warranty length as the manufacturer's own reliability bet
- **Claim:** Signaling theory (game-theoretic economics of asymmetric information) predicts, and multiple empirical marketing studies confirm, that manufacturers who are confident in product reliability offer longer warranties because the expected claim cost is lower for them than for a low-quality competitor mimicking the signal.
- **Mechanism:** A warranty is a real financial liability; a manufacturer only extends it profitably if their internal failure-rate data supports the expected payout being lower than the marketing value of the longer warranty — making warranty length a costly, and therefore harder-to-fake, signal (compared to a marketing claim).
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** Compare a product's *standard-included* warranty length (not optional paid extended warranty) against category norms; a below-category-norm warranty on an otherwise premium-priced product is a red flag.
- **Strength:** Peer-reviewed (multiple marketing/econ journal studies) — but the theory is nuanced.
- **Access:** Paywalled (journal articles behind publisher paywalls, e.g. SAGE/Oxford Academic); working-paper/summary versions sometimes free via ResearchGate.
- **Source(s):** Soberman, "Simultaneous Signaling and Screening with Warranties," *Journal of Marketing Research* 40(2), 2003, https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1509/jmkr.40.2.176.19228; Boulding & Kirmani, "A Consumer-Side Experimental Examination of Signaling Theory," *Journal of Consumer Research* 20(1), 1993, https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-pdf/20/1/111/5370230/20-1-111.pdf.
- **Caveats / false positives:** The signal only works when the *brand's overall reputation is already credible* — empirical work found novices perceive warranty length as a quality signal only for reputable firms, not unknown/low-reputation ones (a long warranty from a fly-by-night brand may be cheap to promise and hard to enforce/claim on in practice — check company registration/financial health, F37/F38, alongside warranty length). Also, independent retailers have a documented incentive to push short-warranty products (to upsell extended service contracts), which can distort what's shown at point of sale, not the manufacturer's actual offer.

### F13. Class-action dockets and court/litigation records as a failure signal
- **Claim:** A pattern of successful or credibly-progressing class-action lawsuits alleging the same defect (e.g., "battery swelling," "screen delamination," "engine oil consumption") against a specific product/model is a strong, hard-to-fake signal of a systemic (not anecdotal) quality problem, because litigation requires named plaintiffs, discovery, and (for certified classes) judicial scrutiny of the underlying factual pattern.
- **Mechanism:** US federal civil litigation is publicly filed on the PACER system; class certification requires a court finding of common facts/questions, i.e. a filter on "is this really a pattern," not just any single complaint.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** Search free PACER mirrors — CourtListener/RECAP (https://www.courtlistener.com), Justia Dockets (federal district/appellate cases, 2004–present), or UniCourt's free crowd-sourced index — for "[product/company name] class action" plus the suspected defect keyword; check case status (filed vs. certified vs. settled vs. dismissed) since a dismissed case is weak evidence and a settled case with a claims process is strong (if indirect, since companies often settle to avoid cost regardless of merit) evidence.
- **Strength:** Regulator-adjacent / primary legal record (judicial filings are primary sources, but case *outcome* quality varies — a filed complaint alone is merely an allegation).
- **Access:** Free via CourtListener/RECAP and Justia; official PACER is paywalled ($0.10/page, capped, first ~150 pages/quarter free under the fee-waiver threshold).
- **Source(s):** CourtListener/RECAP Archive (free), https://www.courtlistener.com; Justia federal docket search (free, 2004–present), via https://libguides.law.asu.edu/federalcourts/docketsandfilings; official PACER, https://pacer.uscourts.gov (fee, but free up to ~150 pages/quarter under the $30 waiver threshold).
- **Caveats / false positives:** The US legal system produces large volumes of nuisance/opportunistic litigation regardless of actual product quality; a *single* lawsuit proves almost nothing. Only treat as signal when: (a) multiple independent suits allege the *same specific defect mechanism*, (b) a class has been certified (judicial gatekeeping passed), or (c) the company settled with a public claims-administration process (implying material exposure). Federal-only via PACER/CourtListener — state-court consumer class actions require separate state-court e-filing portals not covered here.

### F14. EPREL — EU Product Registry for Energy Labelling
- **Claim:** EPREL is the EU's mandatory public database in which every manufacturer must register measured energy-label data (energy class, and category-specific measured parameters like capacity, noise, water use) for regulated product categories (TVs/displays, refrigerators, washing machines, dishwashers, light sources, tyres, and more) before placing them on the EU market.
- **Mechanism:** EU Regulation (2017/1369, the Energy Labelling Framework Regulation) legally requires suppliers to enter compliance-tested data before sale; the label physically on the product carries a QR code linking to the EPREL entry.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** Search EPREL by brand + model identifier or scan the QR code on the physical energy label to pull the manufacturer-submitted-but-legally-binding measured values (energy class, annual kWh, noise dB, capacity) — cross-check this against marketing claims; a mismatch is a compliance violation, not just marketing spin.
- **Strength:** Regulator (European Commission, DG ENER).
- **Access:** Free (public EU database, no login for search).
- **Source(s):** EPREL homepage/search, https://energy-efficient-products.ec.europa.eu/eprel_en; EPREL for suppliers (what must be registered), https://energy-efficient-products.ec.europa.eu/suppliers_en.
- **Caveats / false positives:** Data is supplier-submitted (self-reported under legal liability), not independently pre-verified by the Commission before publication — market-surveillance authorities audit a sample after the fact, so an EPREL entry is "legally binding claim," not "independently lab-tested," though the legal liability materially raises the cost of lying compared to a marketing page.

### F15. EU Energy Label 2021 rescaling — why "A+++" disappeared and what it means for reading old vs new labels
- **Claim:** In March 2021 the EU rescaled its energy-efficiency labels from the old "A+++ to D" scale back to a plain "A to G" scale, because by the end of Feb 2021 over 90% of products in scope had clustered into the old top bands (A+++/A++/A+), making the label useless for differentiating efficient from average products.
- **Mechanism:** Regulation (EU) 2017/1369 mandated the European Commission rescale category-by-category; the new scale is deliberately calibrated so the "A" class starts empty, leaving headroom for future efficiency gains, and models formerly rated A+++ often now show as B or C.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** When comparing a product's energy label to older reviews/marketing referencing "A+++," treat these as **not directly comparable** to a current A-G label — always check the label's issue/registration date via its EPREL QR code before comparing across products bought in different years.
- **Strength:** Regulator (European Commission).
- **Access:** Free (European Commission public web page).
- **Source(s):** European Commission, "In focus: The improved EU energy label," https://commission.europa.eu/news/focus-improved-eu-energy-label-paving-way-more-innovative-and-energy-efficient-products-2021-02-16_en (Feb 16, 2021).
- **Caveats / false positives:** Rescaling rolled out product-category by product-category (not all at once) — as of 2026 further categories (e.g., some monitors) were still transitioning; check the specific product-group timeline on the EPREL suppliers page before assuming a given label is post-rescaling.

### F16. EU Tyre Label — wet grip, rolling resistance, noise, backed by EPREL
- **Claim:** Every tyre sold in the EU must carry a label with three EU-measured performance classes: fuel efficiency/rolling resistance (A-E), wet grip (A-E), and external noise (A-C, plus dB value), with an EPREL QR code and, since the 2021 update, optional snow (3PMSF) and ice-grip pictograms.
- **Mechanism:** Regulation (EU) 2020/740; ratings derive from standardized EU test procedures the manufacturer must run and self-declare (registered in EPREL), subject to market surveillance.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** Look up the tyre's EPREL entry (via QR code or manual brand+model search on the EPREL tyres product group) for the three underlying grades — wet grip in particular correlates with wet-braking distance, a safety-relevant durability-adjacent metric.
- **Strength:** Regulator (European Commission).
- **Access:** Free (EPREL tyres product group, public).
- **Source(s):** EPREL tyres product group, https://energy-efficient-products.ec.europa.eu/product-list/tyres_en; label explainer with methodology summary, https://www.goodyear.eu/en_gb/consumer/learn/eu-tire-label-explained.html.
- **Caveats / false positives:** The label rates only three of many tyre attributes; it says nothing about tread-wear longevity (mileage life), which remains largely a marketing claim without standardized EU disclosure — check independent tyre-wear tests (e.g., ADAC or Auto Bild tyre tests) separately for longevity specifically.

### F17. Indice de réparabilité / indice de durabilité (France) — repairability and durability scoring, official sources
- **Claim:** Since 2021, France requires an `indice de réparabilité` (repairability score /10) on specified electronics categories; since a 2024 decree, this is being progressively replaced category-by-category by a broader `indice de durabilité` (durability index) that adds reliability/robustness/maintainability criteria, starting with TVs (8 Jan 2025) and washing machines (8 Apr 2025).
- **Mechanism:** Manufacturer self-scored against a government-published grid of criteria (documentation availability, disassembly ease, spare-parts price/availability, software-update commitment for durability), published at point of sale; co-developed by the Ministry (via DGCCRF/DGE) with ADEME.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** Check the product page/label for the score /10; for categories where both regimes might apply (transition period), check the specific application date on the ecologie.gouv.fr policy page to know which index legally applies to that product as of its market date.
- **Strength:** Regulator (French Ministry for Ecological Transition + Ministry of Economy).
- **Access:** Free (French government pages and downloadable official documents).
- **Source(s):** economie.gouv.fr, "Tout savoir sur l'indice de durabilité," https://www.economie.gouv.fr/particuliers/mes-droits-conso/bien-consommer/tout-savoir-sur-lindice-de-durabilite; ecologie.gouv.fr, "Indice de durabilité," https://www.ecologie.gouv.fr/politiques-publiques/indice-durabilite (Apr 9, 2024); "Indice de réparabilité," https://www.ecologie.gouv.fr/politiques-publiques/indice-reparabilite.
- **Caveats / false positives:** Scores are manufacturer self-declared (not independently lab-verified before publication) and can be audited/sanctioned after the fact by DGCCRF for false declarations — treat as "legally binding self-report," similar epistemic status to EPREL (F14). iFixit has published a direct comparison noting methodological differences between its own score and the French/EU (JRC) approach — see https://www.ifixit.com/News/80361/ifixit-vs-french-repairability-score and https://www.ifixit.com/News/82023/ifixit-vs-jrc-repairability-score — the two scores can disagree on the same product.

### F18. EU Ecodesign right-to-repair rules (2021) — mandatory spare-parts windows
- **Claim:** Since 1 March 2021 (light sources: 1 Sept 2021), EU Ecodesign implementing regulations require manufacturers/importers of refrigerators, washing machines, dishwashers, and displays (incl. TVs) to make specified spare parts available to professional repairers (and some parts to end users) for a defined minimum period after the last unit is placed on market, delivered within 15 working days, at prices set to be "reasonable," with the parts list published on a freely-accessible manufacturer website.
- **Mechanism:** Product-specific EU Ecodesign implementing regulations under Directive 2009/125/EC, legally binding on any manufacturer selling into the EU regardless of where they are headquartered.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** For any appliance in scope, the agent can check whether the manufacturer publishes the required spare-parts list/ordering page (a legal obligation, meaning its absence is itself a compliance red flag, not just an inconvenience) and compare the stated parts-availability window against the expected product lifetime.
- **Strength:** Regulator (EU, via Ecodesign Directive implementing acts).
- **Access:** Free (European Commission public legal/policy pages).
- **Source(s):** European Commission, "Directive on repair of goods," https://commission.europa.eu/law/law-topic/consumer-protection-law/directive-repair-goods_en; summary of spare-parts/pricing obligations, Lexology, https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=bbcfe09e-cd9e-4426-a9e7-6e1041abafeb (Oct 15, 2019, describing the adopted package effective 2021).
- **Caveats / false positives:** Only covers the specific product categories named in each implementing regulation (not e.g. smartphones directly under this particular Ecodesign package — smartphones/tablets are covered by a separate, later 2025 Ecodesign regulation) — check category coverage before assuming the rule applies.

### F19. ENERGY STAR — US energy-efficiency certification and its verification program
- **Claim:** ENERGY STAR (US EPA + DOE) certifies products meeting energy-efficiency criteria; certification requires third-party-lab testing, and EPA's post-market verification program tests at least 10% of certified basic models annually through EPA-recognized Certification Bodies to catch false claims.
- **Mechanism:** Manufacturer submits test data from an EPA-recognized lab to an EPA-recognized Certification Body, which certifies; EPA then re-tests a statistical sample post-market as a check against gaming.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** Cross-check any "ENERGY STAR certified" claim against the official ENERGY STAR Product Finder database (not just the logo on the box) — a logo without a matching database entry is a red flag.
- **Strength:** Regulator (US EPA, in partnership with DOE).
- **Access:** Free (EPA public database).
- **Source(s):** ENERGY STAR Product Finder, https://www.energystar.gov/products/productstr; verification-program description, https://www.energy.gov/node/773641 ("EPA's verification program focuses on testing of at least 10% of ENERGY STAR certified basic models").
- **Caveats / false positives:** 10% sampling rate means most individual certified models are never independently re-tested in a given cycle — certification reduces but does not eliminate the chance of an inflated claim slipping through for any specific model.

### F20. FCC ID / Equipment Authorization Search — identifying the actual hardware inside a product
- **Claim:** Any device that intentionally emits RF (Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, cellular, etc.) sold in the US must carry an FCC ID, and the FCC's public database (plus mirrors like fccid.io) exposes the certification filing, which often includes internal photos, test reports, and user manuals revealing the actual chipset/module/antenna used — independent of what the manufacturer's marketing states.
- **Mechanism:** Legal requirement under FCC equipment-authorization rules (47 CFR Part 2/15 etc.); filings are made by the manufacturer or the module-maker but reviewed by an FCC-recognized Telecommunication Certification Body (TCB) before grant.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** Find the FCC ID printed on the device or in its regulatory/settings screen, then search it at https://fccid.io or the official FCC advanced search to pull internal teardown photos and confirm the actual wireless module/chipset — useful for verifying "is this really the chip the spec sheet claims" or identifying rebadged/whitebox hardware.
- **Strength:** Regulator (US FCC filing, though searchable mirrors are third-party).
- **Access:** Free (official FCC search); third-party mirrors like fccid.io are also free.
- **Source(s):** Official FCC ID search, https://www.fcc.gov/oet/ea/fccid, advanced search https://apps.fcc.gov/oetcf/eas/reports/GenericSearch.cfm; explainer, Lightfoot Labs, "Using the FCC Database for Embedded Device Recon," https://lightfootlabs.io/blog/fcc-id-lookups/ (Jan 22, 2024).
- **Caveats / false positives:** Products approved under the Supplier's Declaration of Conformity (SDoC) path are NOT required to appear in this database at all — many simple, low-power devices are legitimately absent. Confidentiality requests can suppress internal photos for a period (typically up to 180 days, sometimes longer for specific circumstances) — absence of photos doesn't necessarily mean anything is wrong, check for a "short-term confidentiality" or "permanent confidentiality" request letter among the filing documents.

### F21. UL Product iQ / CE / NF certification lookups — verifying a claimed mark rather than trusting the logo
- **Claim:** UL Solutions' Product iQ database (free, https://productiq.ul.com) lets anyone verify whether a specific product/file number genuinely holds a UL/ETL-type listing, searchable by file number, product category (CCN), or model number — the logo printed on a box or product can be counterfeited or simply absent from a real certification, so the database lookup is the actual verification step, the logo alone is not.
- **Mechanism:** UL issues a File Number at certification; Product iQ mirrors UL's internal certification records and is kept current as UL's authoritative public certification directory.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** Find the UL File Number printed near the UL mark on the product/manual, search it in Product iQ, and confirm the product category and manufacturer name match what's being sold — a mismatch (or no result) is strong evidence of a counterfeit or misapplied mark.
- **Strength:** Vendor methodology / quasi-regulator (UL is a private Nationally Recognized Testing Laboratory (NRTL) recognized by US OSHA, not a government body itself, but its certifications carry regulatory weight in US building/electrical codes).
- **Access:** Free upon registration (UL Product iQ requires a free account to access full certification records).
- **Source(s):** UL Product iQ, https://productiq.ul.com (also mirrored at https://productiq.ulprospector.com/en); explainer, "Finding UL Listed and Certified Fire-Rated Products with UL Product iQ," https://www.ul.com/thecodeauthority/knowledge/ul-listed-certified-database-product-iq.
- **Caveats / false positives:** CE marking (the EU's equivalent) is *self-declared* by the manufacturer (not third-party certified in most cases) and has NO equivalent public verification database — a CE mark alone is much weaker evidence than a UL listing; for CE-marked goods, the agent should instead look for the manufacturer's published EU Declaration of Conformity, which is a legal requirement but not centrally searchable. NF (French standards mark, via AFNOR) requires checking AFNOR Certification's own product-specific mark registries, which are more fragmented than UL's; no single universal NF lookup was found in this research — flag as needing case-by-case verification.

### F22. EU Ecolabel Product Catalogue (ECAT) and EPD registries
- **Claim:** The revamped EU Ecolabel Product Catalogue (ECAT, launched/relaunched with a new interface in 2025) lets anyone search over 100,000 certified goods/services from 3,000+ companies by licence number, product name, or company name, confirming genuine EU Ecolabel certification (a voluntary EU eco-certification requiring third-party verification against category-specific environmental criteria).
- **Mechanism:** Companies apply for EU Ecolabel certification per product category; a Competent Body in an EU member state verifies compliance against published criteria before issuing a licence number, which the catalogue exposes.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** Search the product/company/licence number in ECAT to confirm a claimed EU Ecolabel is real, rather than trusting the flower logo alone.
- **Strength:** Regulator (European Commission oversight, verification delegated to national Competent Bodies).
- **Access:** Free (European Commission public catalogue, no login for basic search).
- **Source(s):** EU Ecolabel Product Catalogue, https://environmental-data.ec.europa.eu/ecolabel/index.html; European Commission announcement, https://environment.ec.europa.eu/news/one-label-more-100000-greener-products-one-catalogue-2025-09-17_en (Sept 17, 2025).
- **Caveats / false positives:** It is the licence holder's own responsibility to register in the catalogue — the Commission's own documentation flags the catalogue may be non-exhaustive, so a real licence *could* in theory be missing from the search results (rare, but means absence-from-catalogue is weaker evidence than presence). Environmental Product Declarations (EPDs) are a separate, distinct instrument (ISO 14025-based, quantified lifecycle environmental data, not a pass/fail label) verified through programme operators like EPD International / the International EPD System (https://www.environdec.com) — no single unified EU government EPD registry was identified in this research; treat multi-operator EPD verification as `UNVERIFIED-STATUS` for a single canonical URL and direct the agent to the specific programme operator named on the EPD document itself.

### F23. EU Cyber Resilience Act (CRA) — mandatory minimum security-support period for connected products
- **Claim:** The EU Cyber Resilience Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/2847) requires manufacturers of "products with digital elements" to define and disclose a security-support period, with a statutory floor of at least 5 years (or the expected product lifetime if shorter) unless the product is expected to be used for less than that — the 5 years is a *minimum*, not a target, and manufacturers must actively report exploited vulnerabilities/incidents (from 11 Sept 2026: 24h early warning, 72h full notification, 14-day final report after fix).
- **Mechanism:** EU regulation binding on any manufacturer placing a connected/digital-element product on the EU market, enforced by national market-surveillance authorities.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** For any connected/IoT product, check the manufacturer's published support-period commitment (should be disclosed per CRA) against the 5-year statutory floor and against typical usage lifetime for that category — a support period shorter than typical product lifetime for a durable good (e.g., a smart thermostat, a router) is a red flag even if CRA-compliant on paper.
- **Strength:** Regulator (EU legislation).
- **Access:** Free (official EU legislative text and Commission guidance pages).
- **Source(s):** European Commission, "Cyber Resilience Act - Manufacturers," https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/cra-manufacturers; support-period explainer, https://bootlin.com/blog/cyber-resilience-act-cra-obligations-for-manufacturers/; incident-reporting timeline, https://www.cyberresilienceact.eu/explained.html.
- **Caveats / false positives:** CRA reporting obligations phase in starting 11 September 2026 — before that date, some obligations are not yet enforceable, so absence of visible compliance in earlier products doesn't necessarily indicate non-compliance with a not-yet-active law. `UNVERIFIED-STATUS`: exact scope boundaries (which product categories count as "digital elements") have documented ambiguity in industry guidance as of 2026; check the latest Commission guidance before treating any specific product as definitively in/out of scope.

### F24. endoflife.date — cross-referenced software/OS/framework support-lifecycle tracker
- **Claim:** endoflife.date is a community-maintained, MIT-licensed, free database tracking 460+ software products' release schedules and official end-of-life/end-of-support dates, aggregated from each vendor's own lifecycle pages, exposed via a simple website and a public REST API.
- **Mechanism:** Volunteer-maintained aggregation with defined data model (per-product YAML/Markdown) and automated update pipelines pulling from vendor sources, git tags, and package registries; over 500 contributors since 2019.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** Query `https://endoflife.date/api/v1/products/<product>` (or the site directly) for a specific OS/language/framework/database version's EOL date; compare against the current date to compute "days until unsupported," a directly actionable number for any software-purchase or dependency decision.

- **Access:** Free (public website and public REST API, no login).
- **Source(s):** endoflife.date homepage, https://endoflife.date/; API docs, https://endoflife.date/docs/api/v1/; project repo, https://github.com/endoflife-date/endoflife.date.
- **Caveats / false positives:** Not a primary source for any single product — always sanity-check the vendor's own lifecycle/EOL page when the decision is high-stakes, since aggregation lag or vendor policy changes can leave the tracker briefly stale.

### F25. NVD/CVE and CVSS — reading vulnerability history without unfairly punishing well-audited software
- **Claim:** The National Vulnerability Database (NVD, US NIST) aggregates CVE records with CVSS severity scores (0.1–3.9 Low, 4.0–6.9 Medium, 7.0–8.9 High, 9.0–10 Critical); a **high raw CVE count is not itself evidence of poor security** — it is frequently the opposite, since well-audited, actively-maintained projects generate more disclosed CVEs precisely because more people are looking for and reporting bugs (a project no one audits has "zero known CVEs" that says nothing about its actual vulnerability count).
- **Mechanism:** CVSS is a technical-severity scoring rubric (impact + exploitability), not a business-risk or trustworthiness score; NVD sometimes must assign "worst case" CVSS values when vendor-disclosed detail is incomplete, artificially inflating perceived severity for some entries.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** When comparing two software options by CVE history, check: (a) CVE *median time-to-patch* (fast patching after disclosure is a positive signal even with many CVEs), (b) whether CVEs are concentrated in old/unsupported versions vs. the current supported branch, (c) whether the project has a published security-disclosure policy/security.txt (indicates a functioning reporting channel), rather than raw CVE count alone.
- **Strength:** Regulator/standards-body-adjacent (NIST is a US federal agency; CVSS itself is maintained by FIRST.org, a non-profit standards body).
- **Access:** Free (US government NVD database, no login).
- **Source(s):** NVD CVSS metrics, https://nvd.nist.gov/vuln-metrics/cvss; on the "worst case" scoring caveat, same NVD page; on the risk of raw-count comparisons, Wiz explainer, "What is CVSS?", https://www.wiz.io/academy/vulnerability-management/what-is-cvss-common-vulnerability-scoring-system.
- **Caveats / false positives:** Vendor-assigned CVSS scores can differ from NVD's own re-scoring for the same CVE (the vendor typically has more context on their own product's actual exposure) — when the two disagree, note both and explain the vendor has more precise product-specific context but also a incentive to downplay severity.

### F26. OpenSSF Scorecard — automated open-source security-practice health checks
- **Claim:** OpenSSF Scorecard runs ~18 automated checks against a GitHub/GitLab repository (branch protection, signed commits, dependency-update automation, presence of a security policy, fuzzing, CI tests, etc.), each scored 0–10, aggregated into a weighted overall score, and is explicitly a *security-practices* score, not a vulnerability scanner.
- **Mechanism:** Static analysis + GitHub/GitLab API queries against repository metadata; language-agnostic; results are published as an open BigQuery dataset covering a large slice of the open-source ecosystem, so many popular packages already have a public score without the agent needing to run the tool itself.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** Look up `https://scorecard.dev` or query the results dataset/badge for a package; a low score on checks like "Maintained" or "Vulnerabilities" is a more targeted risk signal than star count.
- **Strength:** Industry report / OSS-foundation methodology (OpenSSF, under the Linux Foundation; used and endorsed by CISA).
- **Access:** Free (open-source tool; results also published as a free public BigQuery dataset).
- **Source(s):** OpenSSF Scorecard, https://scorecard.dev/ and https://openssf.org/projects/scorecard/; CISA reference, https://www.cisa.gov/resources-tools/services/openssf-scorecard.
- **Caveats / false positives:** Scores checks that correlate with good hygiene, not directly with "this code has no bugs" — a project can score well on process (branch protection, CI) while still shipping a serious logic bug, and vice versa a tiny, correct, feature-complete library can score poorly simply for lacking process overhead it doesn't need.

### F27. deps.dev (Open Source Insights) — dependency-graph and package health aggregation
- **Claim:** Google's deps.dev indexes ~5 million packages across Go, Maven, PyPI, npm, and Cargo, exposing transitive dependency graphs, advisory/vulnerability impact reports, and integrated OpenSSF Scorecard data via a free API/BigQuery dataset — useful for assessing not just a package's own health but its *transitive* dependency risk.
- **Mechanism:** Automated ingestion from each package ecosystem's registry plus advisory databases (e.g., OSV), combined into a graph queryable by package+version.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** Query `https://deps.dev/<ecosystem>/<package>` for a package's full dependency tree and each transitive dependency's known advisories — a package with few direct issues but a deep tree of unmaintained transitive dependencies is higher-risk than the top-level score alone suggests.
- **Strength:** Industry report / vendor tool (Google-run, free, non-commercial in intent).
- **Access:** Free (public web search and free API/BigQuery dataset).
- **Source(s):** deps.dev, https://deps.dev; API docs, https://docs.deps.dev/api/v3/; announcement, https://blog.deps.dev/api/.
- **Caveats / false positives:** Coverage is limited to the five listed ecosystems (Go, Maven, PyPI, npm, Cargo) — does not cover e.g. RubyGems, NuGet, or non-package-manager software at time of research.

### F28. GitHub stars and npm/package download counts are actively manipulated — do not use as standalone signals
- **Claim:** Both metrics are demonstrably gameable at scale in 2024–2026: a large-scale academic study (StarScout) found ~6 million suspected fake GitHub stars across 15,835+ repositories, with fake-star campaigns growing two orders of magnitude in 2024 and at their July 2024 peak accounting for 16% of all repos with star activity; separately, npm's download counter is a naive count of HTTP 200 responses on tarball URLs, independently demonstrated exploitable to fabricate ~1 million spoofed downloads in a week (2021 research, re-validated working in 2026 per Tenable), and actively used in real supply-chain-attack campaigns (e.g., the "ambar-src" campaign inflating a malicious package's apparent legitimacy via ~50,000 fabricated downloads across 724 rapid version releases).
- **Mechanism:** GitHub stars can be purchased from openly-operating star-selling services (Fiverr, Telegram, gray-market forums) using bot accounts; npm downloads can be spoofed by scripting repeated HTTP requests to the package tarball URL, since npm's counter has no bot/dedup filtering by design.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** Cross-check star count against **fork count** — healthy, organically-adopted projects typically show a fork-to-star ratio of 10–25%; a ratio below ~5% on a repo with thousands of stars is a documented anomaly signal (bot accounts star without forking). Cross-check npm downloads against GitHub issue/PR activity and dependent-package count (npm's own "Dependents" tab, or deps.dev's reverse-dependency graph) — real adoption leaves a trail of downstream usage, not just a raw counter.
- **Strength:** Peer-reviewed (StarScout academic paper) + industry report (npm/Tenable technical writeups).
- **Access:** Free (arXiv preprint is open access; npm's own blog and Tenable's research blog are free).
- **Source(s):** "Six Million (Suspected) Fake Stars on GitHub," arXiv:2412.13459, https://arxiv.org/html/2412.13459v2 (updated 2025); Tenable, "Download pumping: How threat actors use new npm deception technique," https://www.tenable.com/blog/how-cyberattackers-inflate-malicious-package-npm-download-counts (May 28, 2026); npm's own admission of the metric's limitations, "numeric precision matters: how npm download counts work," https://blog.npmjs.org/post/92574016600/numeric-precision-matters-how-npm-download-counts-work.html.
- **Caveats / false positives:** Low fork-to-star ratio is a heuristic, not proof — some legitimate use-cases (e.g., a CLI tool people star to bookmark but never fork) also produce low ratios; combine with other signals (issue activity, real-world citations, deps.dev reverse-dependency count) before concluding manipulation.

### F29. Detecting an "abandoned but still popular" open-source project
- **Claim:** No single metric (including "no commits in 12+ months") reliably distinguishes an abandoned project from a stable, feature-complete one; researchers recommend a multi-signal check combining commit recency, issue/PR response latency (especially on security-labeled issues), release-cadence deviation from the project's own historical cadence, whether the maintainer(s) remain active on *other* repositories (indicating deprioritization rather than departure from open source generally), and freshness of the project's own dependencies.
- **Mechanism:** A 2019 academic study found only 41% of projects fully recover maintenance activity after a "Top Few Developers Departure" event, meaning maintainer turnover is a leading indicator of prolonged reduced activity, not just a blip; separately, industry OSSRA reporting found 91% of audited OSS components showed no clear maintenance signal in the trailing two years, indicating the "silently unmaintained but still depended-upon" pattern is the ecosystem norm rather than an edge case.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** Checklist: (1) last commit date vs. project's historical commit-interval baseline (not an absolute threshold); (2) median time-to-first-maintainer-response on the last 10 opened issues; (3) whether the most recent 3 releases follow the project's own historical cadence or show a lengthening gap; (4) whether primary maintainer(s), checked via their GitHub profile, show recent activity elsewhere (active elsewhere = deprioritized, not abandoned-from-OSS; inactive everywhere = higher abandonment risk); (5) whether the project's own `package.json`/`requirements.txt`/`go.mod` dependencies are themselves stale by multiple major versions.
- **Strength:** Peer-reviewed (arXiv study) + industry report (Synopsys OSSRA).
- **Access:** Free (arXiv preprint open access; industry blog summary free).
- **Source(s):** "On the abandonment and survival of open source projects," arXiv:1906.08058, https://arxiv.org/pdf/1906.08058; multi-signal checklist summary, Safeguard, "Abandoned Open Source Project Security Risks," https://safeguard.sh/resources/blog/abandoned-open-source-project-risks.
- **Caveats / false positives:** A cryptography or algorithm-implementation library with no bugs and a stable spec may legitimately go a year+ without a commit while being perfectly safe to use — commit inactivity alone is explicitly *not* sufficient evidence of abandonment; the project's category (does it have an external spec that could change? does it depend on OS/browser APIs that evolve?) matters as much as the raw dates.

### F30. Public status pages and incident postmortems as a SaaS reliability record
- **Claim:** A vendor's public status page (commonly hosted on Statuspage.io, status.io, incident.io, or similar) provides a dated, cumulative incident history — outage start/resolution times, affected components, and (for vendors with a mature practice) a linked post-incident review/postmortem explaining root cause and remediation — that is directly observable without needing the vendor's cooperation and is independently mirrored by third parties (e.g., StatusGator has independently tracked Statuspage.io's own outages since 2015).
- **Mechanism:** The vendor chooses to publish (voluntary transparency practice, not regulatory requirement), but once published the historical record is timestamped and, for platforms using immutable/versioned incident logs, difficult to quietly rewrite after the fact; third-party monitors (StatusGator, IsItDownRightNow-style aggregators) provide an independent cross-check against the vendor's own reporting.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** Check the vendor's status-page history page directly for: incident frequency over the trailing 12 months, mean time-to-resolution, and whether postmortems are published for major incidents (a vendor that publishes root-cause postmortems is demonstrating an operational maturity signal beyond the raw uptime number); cross-check against a third-party outage tracker for the same vendor to catch any gaps in vendor self-reporting.
- **Strength:** Industry report / vendor self-disclosure, partially cross-checkable via independent third-party monitors.
- **Access:** Free (public status pages require no login; third-party trackers like StatusGator are free to browse).
- **Source(s):** Example of vendor incident-history structure, Status.io knowledge base, https://kb.status.io/incidents/incident-overview/; independent cross-check example, StatusGator's own tracking of Statuspage.io outages since 2015, https://statusgator.com/services/statuspageio; on postmortems as a trust signal, https://statusray.com/blog/how-to-write-effective-incident-post-mortems-a-complete-guide.
- **Caveats / false positives:** Status pages are self-reported and self-selected for what counts as an "incident" — vendors vary widely in disclosure rigor, so cross-vendor comparison of "number of incidents" is not apples-to-apples unless definitions are checked; absence of a public status page at all (common for many B2B SaaS tools) is itself a data-availability gap, not evidence of either good or bad reliability.

### F31. Verifying SOC 2 and ISO 27001 claims — there is no public SOC 2 database, but concrete checks exist
- **Claim:** Unlike ISO 27001 (which has accredited-certification-body registries in some jurisdictions), there is **no public database of SOC 2-compliant organizations at all** — SOC 2 reports are private-by-default documents a vendor chooses to share (often under NDA) — so "verification" means checking the *auditor's* credentials and the report's internal consistency, not looking up the company in a registry.
- **Mechanism:** SOC 2 reports must be issued by a CPA-licensed firm under AICPA Trust Services Criteria; the auditor's license status is independently checkable even though the report itself isn't centrally indexed.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** Checklist: (1) verify the named CPA's individual license (not just the firm) at CPAverify.org (a NASBA tool); (2) check the audit firm's AICPA Peer Review "pass" status at the AICPA Peer Review Public File; (3) check the report's opinion type — "unqualified" (clean) vs. "qualified" (exceptions noted, warrants scrutiny); (4) check the audit/observation period length — Type II reports should span at least 6 months (12 is standard) and the report should be less than ~12 months old; (5) check that "Section 3" (system description) is genuinely specific to the company (generic boilerplate reused across unrelated companies is a documented red flag for fraudulent reports); (6) check the Trust Services Criteria actually scoped (Security is mandatory; Availability/Confidentiality/Processing Integrity/Privacy are added based on the vendor's own commitments — a vendor claiming "SOC 2 certified" with no scope detail is itself vague, since SOC 2 has no pass/fail "certification," only an audit opinion).
- **Strength:** Industry report / professional-standards body (AICPA sets Trust Services Criteria; CPA licensing is state-regulated in the US).
- **Access:** Free (CPAverify.org and the AICPA Peer Review Public File are both free public lookups); the SOC 2 report itself is typically shared privately/under NDA by the vendor, not centrally published.
- **Source(s):** CPAverify (NASBA CPA license lookup), referenced in Censinet's verification guide, https://censinet.com/perspectives/verify-soc-2-type-ii-compliance-cloud-vendors-steps; on the absence of a public SOC 2 database, Hicomply, https://www.hicomply.com/en-us/blog/how-to-verify-soc-2-certification; on fraudulent-report red flags (generic Section 3), Lema, https://www.lema.ai/blog/how-to-detect-a-fraudulent-soc-2-report.
- **Caveats / false positives:** "SOC 2 certified" is technically a misnomer commonly used in marketing — SOC 2 produces an *audit opinion*, not a pass/fail certificate; a vendor using the word "certified" loosely isn't necessarily lying, but precise language ("SOC 2 Type II report with an unqualified opinion, covering [dates]") is itself a positive signal of vendor sophistication and directness. ISO 27001, unlike SOC 2, generally IS checkable via the issuing accredited certification body's own public certificate registry (varies by certifying body — e.g., BSI, DNV — check the specific certificate number against the named certification body's registry, not a generic search).

### F32. iFixit Repairability Score methodology (scored rubric, distinct from raw teardown notes in F11)
- **Claim:** iFixit's published Repairability Score (0–10, higher = easier to repair) is built from five equally-weighted-ish criteria: documentation availability/longevity, ease of disassembly, spare-parts availability, spare-parts pricing, and a product-specific criterion (e.g., a battery-swap-specific criterion for phones); disassembly/design difficulty is computed by tracing the actual repair path for each critical component, timing each disassembly/reassembly action and multiplying by a tool-difficulty scaling factor, with design-difficulty components contributing roughly 80% of the overall score and documentation availability another meaningful slice; the rubric is revised at least annually to account for new failure modes like parts-pairing/calibration lockouts.
- **Mechanism:** iFixit engineers physically disassemble and reassemble the product, logging every step against a published, versioned rubric (v1.1 through v2.0+ are all publicly archived, so scores from different rubric versions are not directly comparable).
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** A score ≥5/10 is calibrated by iFixit as "you can probably fix this yourself"; below 5 shifts toward "call a pro"; 0/10 typically indicates catastrophically destructive or impossible disassembly. Always check which rubric *version* a given score was computed under before comparing two products' scores directly.
- **Strength:** Vendor methodology (iFixit is a repair-parts retailer with a commercial interest in repairability being valued, but the rubric and raw teardown evidence are public and falsifiable).
- **Access:** Free (public methodology pages and versioned rubric archive).
- **Source(s):** "How Is the iFixit Repairability Score Calculated?", https://www.ifixit.com/News/75533/how-ifixit-scores-repairability; versioned rubric archive, e.g. https://www.ifixit.com/Wiki/Repairability_Scoring_Rubric_v2.0.
- **Caveats / false positives:** Scores are not always populated for every model in a category (only products iFixit chooses to tear down); comparing an iFixit-scored model against an un-scored competitor tells the agent nothing about the competitor.

### F33. Right-to-repair legal status — jurisdiction-specific, check both the US state and the EU/France separately
- **Claim:** As of 2026, six US states have enacted right-to-repair laws for consumer electronics (New York, California, Oregon, Colorado, Minnesota, Massachusetts), with Connecticut and Texas adding elements later in 2026, covering roughly a quarter of the US population; there is still no comprehensive US federal right-to-repair statute for digital electronics. Separately, the EU's Right to Repair Directive (2024/1799) started applying 31 July 2026 EU-wide, extending the legal-guarantee period by an extra year when a consumer chooses repair over replacement (previously a 6-month extension) and protecting independent repairers' access to 3D-printed/secondhand/compatible spare parts; new EU rules specifically for smartphones/tablets took effect 20 June 2025.
- **Mechanism:** Patchwork of state legislation in the US (no federal mandate); a single EU directive that member states (including France) must transpose into national law, layered on top of pre-existing Ecodesign spare-parts rules (F18).
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** When assessing a product's repair prospects, check whether the *state* (US) or *EU membership* (post-31 July 2026) the consumer is in has an applicable right-to-repair law in force for that specific product category — coverage is not uniform (e.g., Oregon's parts-pairing ban explicitly excludes video game consoles, medical devices, solar panels, agricultural equipment).
- **Strength:** Regulator (state/EU legislation).
- **Access:** Free (public trackers and official legislative/EUR-Lex text).
- **Source(s):** State-by-state tracker, https://consumerelectronicsdaily.com/right-to-repair/right-to-repair-laws-us-tracker/; PIRG, "More than one-quarter of Americans covered by Right to Repair come Jan. 1," https://pirg.org/articles/more-than-one-quarter-of-americans-covered-by-right-to-repair-come-jan-1/ (Dec 26, 2025); EU Directive summary, EUR-Lex, https://eur-lex.europa.eu/EN/legal-content/summary/common-rules-promoting-the-repair-of-goods-and-amending-related-eu-legislation.html; France-specific extended-guarantee mechanics, https://www.connexionfrance.com/practical/how-does-frances-new-right-to-repair-rule-cover-your-purchases/807600.
- **Caveats / false positives:** `UNVERIFIED-STATUS` flag: right-to-repair legislation is moving fast (multiple new state laws activated exactly at the 2026-01-01 boundary, EU directive newly applying 2026-07-31) — any claim about current legal status should be re-checked against the cited trackers for the specific product category and jurisdiction, since this is one of the fastest-moving regulatory areas in this entire research slice.

### F34. Secondhand resale price after N years as a crowd-sourced durability verdict
- **Claim:** Resale/depreciation curves (observable via completed/sold listings on marketplaces like eBay) are a legitimate, market-aggregated proxy for perceived long-term durability and reliability, since buyers price in expected remaining useful life, but the signal is confounded by brand desirability, fashion/design-trend effects, and market demand independent of actual durability (e.g., a stylish-but-fragile item can hold value via demand even if it fails often).
- **Mechanism:** Marketplace observers recommend using recency-weighted, condition-filtered comparable sold listings (8–15 comparable "sold" transactions, condition-matched) rather than any single listing, since asking prices (not sold prices) are not evidence of actual market value.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** On eBay, filter to "Sold Listings" (not active listings) for the specific model/condition, collect 8–15 recent comparable sales, and compute resale value as a fraction of original MSRP at a given age — a category-relative comparison (this model retains X% of value at 3 years vs. Y% for a direct competitor) is more informative than an absolute number.
- **Strength:** Industry report / market-observation methodology (not academic, but a widely-used practitioner heuristic with documented rationale).
- **Access:** Free to browse eBay sold listings (registration required only to sell, not to search/view).
- **Source(s):** eBay sold-listings valuation methodology, https://resellbot.com/ebay-sold-listings/ and https://www.underpriced.app/blog/how-to-use-ebay-sold-listings-price-research-guide; resale-value formula and durability/brand-reliability linkage, https://www.wallstreetprep.com/knowledge/resale-value/ and https://www.kamdhenucars.com/blog/decoding-depreciation-why-luxury-cars-hold-their-value-in-the-pre-owned-market-48.
- **Caveats / false positives:** High resale value is NOT proof of high reliability by itself — it can equally reflect brand cachet, artificial scarcity, or fashion; conversely low resale value can reflect fast technological obsolescence (irrelevant to build quality) rather than unreliability. Use resale value as a *corroborating* signal alongside F1–F13, never as standalone evidence, and explicitly note the confound when citing it.

### F35. Total cost of ownership / consumable lock-in as an independently computable quality-of-decision factor
- **Claim:** For products with a "razor-and-blade" business model (inkjet printers being the canonical, well-documented example), the sticker price is a poor predictor of total cost, and this is independently and precisely computable by the agent from published consumable pricing without needing any trust/quality judgment at all — e.g., a ~$60 inkjet printer with ~$148/year ink cost reaches ~$800 total cost over 5 years, versus a ~$350 ink-tank printer at ~$5/year ink reaching ~$375 over 5 years (a documented ~2x total-cost reversal despite the ink-tank model costing ~6x more upfront).
- **Mechanism:** Manufacturers deliberately price hardware near or below cost and recoup margin on locked-in consumables (often DRM-enforced via cartridge microchips), a well-documented deliberate business strategy, not an incidental cost structure.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** For any product category with recurring locked consumables (printer ink/toner, coffee-machine pods, razor blades, subscription-gated features, proprietary batteries), compute total cost over the expected ownership horizon = purchase price + (consumable unit cost × expected consumption rate × years), and compare across models on that basis rather than sticker price.
- **Strength:** Industry report / consumer-advocacy analysis with directly verifiable underlying prices (this is arithmetic on public price data, essentially self-verifying once the agent pulls the actual current consumable prices).
- **Access:** Free (public consumer-advocacy pricing analysis and manufacturer-published consumable prices).
- **Source(s):** Consumer Reports, "The high cost of wasted printer ink" / cheapest-printers-for-ink-costs analysis with the $800-vs-$375 five-year example, https://www.consumerreports.org/electronics-computers/printers/cheapest-printers-for-ink-costs-a6953539933/; on the deliberate business-model framing, https://blog.tonercartridgedepot.com/2025/07/15/why-ink-costs-more-than-gold-hp-epson-lexmark-or-xerox-for-top-quality-and-savings/; original ink-vs-champagne price-per-ml comparison, The Drinks Business, https://www.thedrinksbusiness.com/2019/07/printer-ink-costs-10-times-more-than-vintage-champagne/ (Jul 23, 2019, citing Which?).
- **Caveats / false positives:** Consumable prices and consumption-rate assumptions change over time and vary by actual usage pattern (a low-volume user may never recoup an ink-tank printer's higher upfront cost) — the agent should compute the TCO crossover point (break-even usage volume) rather than assert a single winner, and always re-pull current consumable prices rather than relying on cited historical figures.

### F36. US state contractor-licence-board lookups (worked via California CSLB)
- **Claim:** US contractor licensing is state-regulated; California's Contractors State License Board (CSLB) provides a free public lookup by license number, business name, or owner name exposing license status, trade classification(s), issue/expiry dates, bond status, workers'-comp status, and complaint-disclosure history — and critically, bond/workers'-comp suspensions happen automatically and silently the day coverage lapses, with no proactive warning to consumers or even to the general contractor hiring a sub.
- **Mechanism:** Statutory licensing regime; CSLB requires a $25,000 surety bond (from a California-DOI-licensed surety) as a condition of active licensure, and continuously monitors bond/insurance lapses, auto-suspending the license on lapse.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** Look up the contractor at https://www2.cslb.ca.gov/onlineservices/checklicenseii/CheckLicense.aspx by license number/name; confirm license status is "Active" (not "Suspended" or "Expired"), that the trade classification matches the work being quoted, and that bond and workers'-comp fields show current coverage, not just "license exists."
- **Strength:** Regulator (California state licensing board; every US state has an equivalent, structure varies).
- **Access:** Free (California CSLB public lookup, no login).
- **Source(s):** CSLB Check A License, https://www2.cslb.ca.gov/onlineservices/checklicenseii/CheckLicense.aspx; bond requirements, https://www.cslb.ca.gov/contractors/maintain_license/bond_information/bond_requirements.aspx.
- **Caveats / false positives:** Every US state runs its own separate board with its own URL/lookup format (this is a worked example for California specifically, not a national database) — the agent must find the correct state licensing board for the contractor's actual jurisdiction. A currently-active license says nothing about a lapse that happened and was cured before the check — check the license's full status history/complaint disclosure, not just the current-moment snapshot.

### F37. Companies House (UK) — filing history, insolvency status, and winding-up petitions
- **Claim:** Companies House's free "Find and update company information" service exposes UK company filing history, registered officers, and an explicit insolvency tab; because there is a documented lag (Companies House filings can lag actual events; The Gazette—the UK's official public-notice publication—usually shows winding-up petitions within about 7 days) a thorough check requires cross-referencing Companies House AND The Gazette (for winding-up petitions) AND the Individual Insolvency Register (for sole traders/individual bankruptcy).
- **Mechanism:** UK statutory company-register requirements plus mandatory public notice of insolvency proceedings in The London Gazette.
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** Search the contractor/company name at https://find-and-update.company-information.service.gov.uk/, check the "insolvency" tab and filing history for recency/gaps (a company that has stopped filing accounts on schedule is itself a distress signal even absent formal insolvency), then cross-check https://www.thegazette.co.uk for any pending winding-up petition not yet reflected at Companies House.
- **Strength:** Regulator (UK government, Companies House + The Gazette, both official).
- **Access:** Free (Companies House and The Gazette are both free UK government services).
- **Source(s):** Companies House search, https://find-and-update.company-information.service.gov.uk/; Gazette winding-up notice explainer, https://go-legal.co.uk/winding-up-petition-search-uk-how-to-check-insolvency-status-online/; lag caveat, https://www.globalx.co/court-services/company-insolvency-and-administration-search/.
- **Caveats / false positives:** A clean Companies House record is *weaker* evidence than a red flag due to the reporting lag — treat "nothing found" as "no confirmed issue as of the last filing," not "confirmed healthy."

### F38. Pappers.fr / BODACC / INPI (France) — company registry and financial-distress signals
- **Claim:** Pappers.fr aggregates official French sources (INSEE for SIREN/SIRET/NAF identification, INPI via data.inpi.fr for corporate deeds/appointments, BODACC for creation/modification/cessation notices and collective insolvency proceedings, and commercial-court greffes for filed annual accounts) into a single free (for browsing; paid API for bulk/technical integration) searchable interface; Pappers itself produces no data — it is an aggregator layer over primary government sources.
- **Mechanism:** BODACC (Bulletin Officiel des Annonces Civiles et Commerciales) is the statutorily mandated official publication channel for French company registrations, modifications, and — critically — collective insolvency procedures (safeguard/redressement/liquidation judiciaire).
- **Agent-observable signal / formula:** Search the SIREN/company name at https://www.pappers.fr, filter to "Procédures collectives" and review at least the last 3 years for any safeguard/restructuring/liquidation filing; for the canonical primary source (bypassing the aggregator), check https://www.bodacc.fr directly and https://data.inpi.fr for the underlying filings.
- **Strength:** Aggregator over regulator sources (BODACC/INPI/INSEE are official; Pappers is a private free-access aggregator layer, not itself a government body).
- **Access:** Free for browsing company files/accounts/events on pappers.fr; the Pappers API (bulk/technical integration) is paywalled starting at €20/month. BODACC and data.inpi.fr themselves are free.
- **Source(s):** Aggregation-methodology description, https://hayot-expertise.fr/en/blog/pappers-company-data-tool-review; BODACC official description, European e-Justice Portal, https://e-justice.europa.eu/topics/registers-business-insolvency-land/business-registers-eu-countries/fr_en; financial-distress lookup how-to, https://www.busiboost.fr/entreprises-en-difficulte-comment-les-reperer-a-temps-grace-a-pappers/.
- **Caveats / false positives:** Documented limitations: update delays, partial financial-account disclosure since France's PACTE law (2019) raised disclosure thresholds for small companies, and no built-in risk-scoring/rating — the agent must interpret raw filings itself rather than trust any single "risk score" a third-party tool might layer on top.

### F39. PACER / CourtListener / UniCourt — US federal court records as company/contractor due-diligence (cross-reference with F13)
- See F13 for the full detail on using these sources to detect litigation patterns; the same infrastructure applies directly to vetting a contractor or company for prior lawsuits (breach of contract, negligence, consumer-protection actions), not just consumer-product class actions. CourtListener/RECAP (free) and Justia (free, federal only, 2004–present) are the most cost-effective entry points before resorting to paid PACER access ($0.10/page, capped, with a fee waiver for the first ~150 pages/quarter).
- **Source(s):** https://www.courtlistener.com; https://pacer.uscourts.gov/my-account-billing/billing/options-access-records-if-you-cannot-afford-pacer-fees.
- **Access:** Free via CourtListener/RECAP and Justia; official PACER is paywalled ($0.10/page, capped, first ~150 pages/quarter free under the fee-waiver threshold) — see F13 access note.

## Operational checklist candidates

1. Before trusting any star rating, identify the product category and route to the matching primary source in this file (F1–F39) rather than reading further reviews. (F1–F39)
2. For any durable hardware item (drive, GPU, appliance, vehicle), pull the relevant large-N operator/inspection dataset (Backblaze, Puget, TÜV, ADAC, Stiftung Warentest, Which?) and read the *category baseline* (typical time-to-first-failure) before judging whether the specific product's claimed lifespan is plausible. (F1, F2, F4–F7)
3. For any vehicle, check NHTSA (US) or the equivalent EU national type-approval/recall register by VIN/model, and separately pull TÜV/ADAC-style inspection data if in a market that publishes it, rather than relying solely on JD Power-style survey scores. (F6–F9)
4. Always discount JD Power-style PP100-based scores for known flaws: no severity weighting, brand-level aggregation, no post-warranty data — cite the specific caveat, don't just report the number. (F8)
5. For any EU-regulated product (appliance, TV, tyre, light source), pull the EPREL entry via the physical label's QR code and compare the measured values against marketing claims; treat any mismatch as a compliance signal, not just marketing spin. (F14–F16)
6. For any product sold in France in an in-scope category, check both the repairability/durability index score AND its rubric version/date, since two regimes (réparabilité vs durabilité) are running in parallel through the 2025–2026 transition. (F17)
7. Before treating a UL/CE/NF/ENERGY STAR/Ecolabel logo as evidence, look up the certification/file/licence number in the issuing body's own database — a logo alone is not verification. (F19, F21, F22)
8. For any RF-emitting electronic device, pull its FCC ID filing (if it has one) to inspect real internals/chipset against marketing claims, noting that SDoC-path devices legitimately won't appear. (F20)
9. For any connected/IoT product, check the manufacturer's disclosed security-support period against the EU CRA's 5-year statutory floor and against the product's expected physical lifetime, flagging any gap. (F23)
10. For any software dependency or platform, check endoflife.date for the support-window countdown before recommending or building on it. (F24)
11. When evaluating a codebase's CVE history, read patch latency and current-vs-legacy-branch concentration, not raw CVE count — a high count on an actively-patched, well-audited project is not itself bad. (F25)
12. For open-source packages, pull OpenSSF Scorecard and deps.dev data instead of relying on star count or download count alone, and explicitly check the fork-to-star ratio and reverse-dependency count as anti-manipulation checks. (F26–F28)
13. Before declaring an open-source project "abandoned," run the full multi-signal check (commit cadence vs. own baseline, issue-response latency, maintainer activity elsewhere, dependency freshness) — never conclude from commit-date alone. (F29)
14. For any SaaS/cloud tool, pull its public status page's incident history and check for published postmortems on major incidents; cross-check against an independent outage tracker if available. (F30)
15. Before accepting a "SOC 2 certified" or "ISO 27001 certified" claim, verify the named auditor's CPA license (CPAverify) for SOC 2, or the specific certificate number against the named certifying body's registry for ISO 27001 — do not accept the claim on the vendor's word alone. (F31)
16. For repairability comparisons, cite the iFixit score's rubric version explicitly and never compare scores computed under different rubric versions as if equivalent. (F32)
17. Before asserting a right-to-repair protection applies, check both the specific jurisdiction (US state or EU/France) AND the specific product category against the current tracker, since this area is changing month to month as of 2026. (F33)
18. When using resale value as a durability signal, pull condition-filtered sold (not asking) comparables and explicitly flag the brand-desirability/fashion confound rather than presenting resale value as pure durability evidence. (F34)
19. For any product with a locked-consumable business model, compute total cost of ownership over the expected use horizon using current published consumable prices, and report the break-even usage point, not just a single "winner." (F35)
20. Before hiring any licensed tradesperson, verify license status (not just existence), bond/insurance currency, and complaint history via the specific state/country licensing board — treat a currently-active license as a point-in-time snapshot, not a guarantee. (F36)
21. Before engaging any company/contractor entity, cross-reference the national company registry (Companies House / Pappers-BODACC-INPI / equivalent) AND, where available, court-record search (CourtListener/PACER) for insolvency proceedings or a pattern of unresolved litigation — treat a clean record as weaker evidence than a flagged one, given documented reporting lags. (F37–F39)

## Worked mini-examples: question → chain → primary source

### (a) A laptop
Question: "Is the [Brand X 14-inch laptop, model Y] actually reliable, and can I fix it myself if it breaks?"
Chain: (1) Pull its energy-label/eco data if sold in the EU via EPREL by brand+model (F14) to confirm displayed specs are legally-binding, not just marketing. (2) Search iFixit for a teardown of the exact model (F11) and, if scored, its Repairability Score with rubric version (F32) — a soldered-RAM, glued-battery result predicts both harder DIY repair and likely-higher OEM repair cost. (3) Search the model's FCC ID (printed under the laptop or in regulatory settings) to confirm the actual Wi-Fi/Bluetooth module matches spec-sheet claims (F20). (4) Check the manufacturer's published warranty length against category norms as a secondary signal, only meaningful if the brand is already reputable (F12). (5) If assessing longevity economics, check eBay sold listings for the same model at 2–3 years old as a resale-value cross-check (F34), and check whether the jurisdiction's right-to-repair law (F33) covers laptops as of the purchase date. No single step is sufficient alone; the EPREL+iFixit+FCC ID triangulation is the closest thing to "hard data" available before ever reading a review.

### (b) A dishwasher
Question: "Will this dishwasher actually last 10 years, and is the 'A' energy rating meaningful?"
Chain: (1) Scan the physical EU energy label's QR code or search EPREL directly by brand+model (F14) — confirm this is a post-March-2021 rescaled A-G label, not a legacy A+++ claim being mis-displayed (F15). (2) Check France's `indice de durabilité` (if sold in France, dishwashers began transition 2025 per the ecologie.gouv.fr schedule) or `indice de réparabilité` score depending on transition date (F17). (3) Cross-check the EU Ecodesign spare-parts obligation (F18) — the manufacturer is legally required to publish a spare-parts list/ordering page; its absence is a compliance red flag, not just an inconvenience. (4) Pull Stiftung Warentest's dishwasher reliability survey data for category base-rate time-to-first-failure (F4) to sanity-check any specific-model claim. (5) Search Rappel Conso / EU Safety Gate / CPSC for this brand+model for any safety recall (F10).

### (c) A SaaS tool
Question: "Is this project-management SaaS actually reliable and well-run, or is it one incident away from losing my data?"
Chain: (1) Pull the vendor's public status page and read the last 12 months of incident history plus whether major incidents have linked postmortems (F30). (2) Cross-check against an independent outage tracker (e.g., StatusGator-style third party) for gaps in vendor self-reporting (F30). (3) If the vendor claims SOC 2 or ISO 27001, verify: for SOC 2, look up the named CPA's license on CPAverify.org and the audit firm's AICPA Peer Review status, and check the report's opinion type/period/Section-3 specificity (F31); for ISO 27001, check the certificate number against the specific issuing certification body's own registry. (4) Check the vendor's published data-processing-agreement/subprocessor list for undisclosed fourth-party dependencies (not separately sourced in this file — flag as an open item if not found on the vendor's trust/legal page). (5) Check whether the vendor discloses a security-update/EOL policy for any self-hosted or on-prem component via endoflife.date (F24) or its own docs, and whether the EU CRA support-period floor (F23) is relevant if the tool is sold with digital-element hardware.

### (d) An npm package
Question: "Should I add this npm package as a dependency, or is it secretly abandoned/gamed?"
Chain: (1) Do NOT start from GitHub star count or npm weekly-download count alone — both are cheaply manipulated (F28); instead pull the OpenSSF Scorecard for the repo (F26) and its deps.dev entry (F27) for aggregated security-practice and dependency-health signals. (2) Run the abandonment multi-signal checklist (F29): commit cadence vs. the project's own historical baseline, response latency on the last 10 issues, release-cadence trend, whether the maintainer is active elsewhere on GitHub, and whether the package's own dependencies are stale. (3) Cross-check the fork-to-star ratio (10–25% = healthy; <5% on a high-star repo = anomaly worth investigating) (F28). (4) Check its CVE/NVD history (F25) for patch latency and whether CVEs cluster in old, no-longer-supported versions vs. the currently-recommended version — do not penalize a package purely for having disclosed CVEs if they were patched quickly. (5) Check endoflife.date (F24) if the package tracks a versioned support policy (e.g., a framework, not a small utility library).

### (e) A roofing contractor
Question: "Can I trust this roofing contractor to finish the job and honor the workmanship warranty?"
Chain: (1) Look up the contractor's license at the relevant US state licensing board (worked example: California CSLB, F36) — confirm status is "Active" (not suspended/expired), that the trade classification matches roofing work, and that bond and workers'-comp coverage are current as of today, not just "exists." (2) If in the UK, search Companies House for the contracting company's filing history and insolvency tab, and cross-check The Gazette for any pending winding-up petition not yet reflected (F37). (3) If in France, search Pappers/BODACC for the SIREN, filtering to "Procédures collectives" for the last 3 years, and check filing recency as a distress signal (F38). (4) Search CourtListener/PACER (US) or the equivalent for a pattern (not a single instance) of breach-of-contract or negligence litigation naming the specific company, treating a certified class or a settlement with a claims process as stronger evidence than a single filed complaint (F13, F39). (5) Treat a clean record on all of the above as weaker evidence than a red flag, given the documented reporting lags in every one of these registries (F37, F38).

## Dead ends and myths

- **"Mindfactory publishes GPU failure/RMA rates"** — Currently unverifiable; the only live, dated Mindfactory data found in this research (3DCenter.org's quarterly reports) is unit-sales volume, not defect or return-rate data. Circulating "Mindfactory Ausfallquote" charts should be treated as unsourced/stale unless traced to a specific dated release. `UNVERIFIED-STATUS` (F3).
- **"A CE mark works like a UL listing and can be database-verified the same way"** — False. CE marking in the EU is manufacturer self-declared with no equivalent centralized public verification database; a UL listing (F21) has a proper searchable registry, CE marking generally does not.
- **"High GitHub star count or npm download count = adopted and trustworthy"** — Directly contradicted by 2024–2026 evidence of large-scale, cheap manipulation of both metrics (F28); low correlation (r≈0.14–0.33) has also been found empirically between stars and downloads even absent manipulation, meaning the two "popularity" metrics don't even agree with each other on unmanipulated projects.
- **"No commits in the last year means the project is dead"** — Contradicted by explicit expert guidance: a feature-complete, correct library can legitimately go quiet; commit-date alone is insufficient without checking issue-response latency, maintainer activity elsewhere, and dependency freshness (F29).
- **"A clean Companies House / Pappers / court-records check means the contractor is financially healthy"** — Overstated. All these registries have a documented reporting lag (Companies House: filings lag events; Gazette: ~7 days for winding-up notices; Pappers: inherits INPI/greffe filing delays plus post-PACTE-law partial disclosure) — absence of a red flag is weaker evidence than presence of one (F37, F38).
- **"SOC 2 certified" is a pass/fail certification like ISO 27001** — Technically incorrect; SOC 2 produces an audit *opinion* (unqualified/qualified), not a certificate, and there is no public "is this company SOC 2 compliant" database at all — the correct verification path is checking the named CPA's license and the report's internal details, not looking up the company (F31).
- **"A high star warranty/rating on a no-name brand means the same as on an established brand"** — Contradicted by the empirical marketing-signaling literature: warranty length (and by extension possibly other self-declared quality signals) is only reliably informative when paired with an already-credible brand reputation (F12).
